This is the first equal-rubric tournament pass for the four Phase-1 entrants. It freezes each entrant's smallest plausible product identity before scoring, attacks its normal-play hook, 20-minute demo, mature reasoning families, campaign burden, dominant strategies, accessibility/readability, implementation ambiguity and current market distance. One entrant is eliminated only because new destructive evidence makes its risk materially worse; no winner is forced in Run 1.

---

# 1. Equal tournament rubric

Each entrant is scored 0–10 on the same ten dimensions. Scores are pressure indicators, not proof of fun.

1. **10-second mute hook** — can normal play communicate a specific cause/effect without captions?
2. **Repeated 5–15 second feel** — is the signature verb satisfying often enough to carry minute-to-minute play?
3. **Systemic depth** — can a small rule vocabulary produce distinct reasoning families rather than one trick with new skins?
4. **20-minute demo strength** — can the real differentiator appear, be independently used, and culminate inside a commercial demo?
5. **Current market distance** — how specific/ownable is the product position after fresh 2026 nearest-neighbor checks?
6. **Production safety** — can the product be built deterministically without disproportionate physics/AI/tooling risk?
7. **Content efficiency** — can 4–8 hours be authored from reusable rules rather than bespoke consumer/mechanism inflation?
8. **Accessibility/readability** — can controller, low-motion, color-independent and low-explanation play remain legible?
9. **Portfolio distance** — does it avoid reusing the factory's prior central fantasies/mechanics?
10. **Cheap falsifiability** — can one week of graybox work decisively kill or validate the thesis?

A high total does not immunize a candidate from a fatal direct-precedent or product-identity problem.

# 3. G5C02 — Tension Budget

## Frozen tournament pitch
**Move one physical anchor and watch a fixed tension budget redistribute across connected machinery, solving traversal by deciding which loads should be slack, taut or highly tensioned at the same time.**

## Exact smallest vocabulary
- one player-controlled physical anchor per encounter;
- anchor constrained to one authored rail or 3–5 sockets;
- 2–4 visible cable-connected loads;
- each cable has only three canonical states: **SLACK / TAUT / HIGH**;
- each load has one visible response curve tied to those states;
- anchor relocation deterministically redistributes the fixed encounter tension state;
- mature content may visibly add/remove one load through an ordinary objective action;
- no rope physics authority, no free cable construction, no numeric tension requirement, no detached graph UI.

## Normal-play 10-second mute trailer trace
0–2s: player reaches the anchor and grabs it.
2–5s: player slides it one socket right; cable A straightens, lift rises, cable B sags, a counterweighted gate drops.
5–7s: player immediately runs onto the newly raised lift.
7–10s: camera keeps both collateral mechanisms visible as the player rides upward toward a route that now exists while the old doorway is visibly gone.

The hook is causal without text if cable posture and load motion are visually strong.

## 20-minute demo paper trace
**0–4 min — One cable / one load.** Move anchor and see a platform change. No failure pressure.

**4–8 min — First tradeoff.** Two loads respond oppositely. Player independently chooses a socket to open a path.

**8–13 min — Physical traversal coupling.** Correct distribution creates geometry the player must personally traverse before the next anchor access.

**13–17 min — Three-load compromise.** The best state is not max/min; player must read three physical consequences.

**17–20 min — Load mutation / extraction.** Objective visibly removes one counterweight. The entry socket now produces a different distribution; player must make a second, newly reasoned anchor choice to exit.

Differentiator reveal is complete by the demo end without adding another system.

## Distinct mature reasoning families
1. **Collateral tradeoff** — improving one load worsens another.
2. **Threshold compromise** — useful answer is a middle distribution, not an extreme.
3. **Traversal coupling** — player position makes the same distribution useful at one moment and useless later.
4. **Sequence preservation** — 2–4 anchor placements form one solution; no permanent best socket.
5. **Topology/load mutation** — objective adds/removes a load and changes later consequences.
6. **Return inversion** — entry configuration becomes wrong for extraction.
7. **Equivalent-local / different-global** — two sockets solve the obvious nearby load, but only one preserves a later route.

## 4–8 hour campaign burden
Plausible with roughly:
- 3–4 reusable load archetypes;
- 3–5 anchor sockets per encounter;
- 2–4 simultaneous loads;
- 25–35 main encounters plus optional mastery/remixes if Phase 5 later supports them.

No fifth/sixth exotic load should be added merely to create variety. If three reusable load families cannot support mature diversity, the concept should be killed rather than rescued with gadgets.

## Destructive attack
**Dominant strategy:** enumerate all sockets until the route looks open.

Defense must come from physical sequencing and state mutation, not hidden information. Exact visible consequences remain allowed; mature rooms fail if one static safe anchor station lets the player cycle every socket and directly reveal the complete solution.

**Waiting:** should be negligible. Moving machinery may have short deterministic transitions but no patrol-cycle waiting.

**Execution burden:** low if sockets commit discrete states. Dragging must be quick and tactile; no precision rope placement.

**UI abstraction:** fatal if players need a cable diagram or numbers to understand which loads changed.

**Hour-5 repetition:** strongest unresolved risk is `move anchor, watch several things change` becoming visually different but cognitively same. Run 2 should attack whether the seven reasoning families truly remain distinct with only 3–4 load archetypes.

## Accessibility/readability
Good controller shape: approach anchor, grab, move along one axis/sockets, release. Cable states need redundant shape/animation patterns rather than color-only signaling. Reduced-motion mode can snap load positions after a short transition while preserving causal highlighting.

## One-week kill prototype
Graybox one facility with:
- 4 sockets;
- 3 cables;
- lift, gate and weighted arm;
- one objective that removes a load;
- exact nonnumeric posture feedback.

**PASS if:**
- unfamiliar players predict at least 70–80% of socket consequences after the teaching state without asking for tension numbers;
- at least one mature puzzle is solved by reasoning about 2+ consequences, not blind enumeration;
- moving the anchor feels like a physical action rather than selecting a menu state;
- no rope/physics simulation is needed for believable play.

**FAIL if:**
- players repeatedly ask for gauges/numbers/graph UI;
- trial-every-socket is consistently faster/easier than reading the world;
- cable/load state is visually ambiguous at normal camera scale;
- the signature action feels like a slow switch selector.

# 4. G5C37 — Zero-G Tool Orbit

## Frozen tournament pitch
**Throw one carried tool onto a predictable local orbit track, let it keep acting periodically while you move elsewhere, then catch and hand it to another anchor to solve the room as two coordinated moving actors.**

## Exact smallest vocabulary
- top-down compact zero-g rooms;
- simple player thrust/movement with strong damping/capped velocity; no realistic-flight requirement;
- exactly one active carried tool baseline;
- authored orbit anchors with 2–3 visible tracks each;
- release inside a capture zone snaps tool to a previewed deterministic track and phase;
- tool repeats the track until caught/transferred;
- orbiting tool can strike marked momentary targets, intercept one marked hazard family, and enter catch/transfer zones;
- catch windows are generous and deterministic;
- no free gravitational n-body simulation, no inventory/tool catalogue required for baseline.

## Normal-play 10-second mute trailer trace
0–2s: player approaches anchor with wrench; two orbit tracks illuminate.
2–4s: player releases wrench to outer track; wrench begins a clean repeating loop.
4–6s: wrench strikes a switch, door opens; player immediately thrusts through.
6–8s: wrench continues orbiting behind/alongside player and hits the switch again on its next cycle.
8–10s: player reaches the opposite arc, catches the still-moving wrench, and the door state changes as the periodic actor disappears into the player's hand.

This is the tournament's strongest purely visual clip if authored track entry is readable.

## 20-minute demo paper trace
**0–4 min — First orbit.** Tool circles one anchor and repeatedly hits one target. Player crosses a simple door.

**4–8 min — Track choice.** Inner/outer track selects which of two marked targets is struck.

**8–13 min — Independent movement.** Launch tool, move across the room while it continues acting, catch on the far side.

**13–17 min — Hand-off.** Catch from anchor A and release into anchor B; player must choose where to be when the tool arrives.

**17–20 min — State mutation / return.** Objective rotates a blocker/target relation, making the original orbit harmful. Player catches and reassigns the tool for extraction.

The actual differentiator — persistent second actor + catch/transfer — appears well before the demo end.

## Distinct mature reasoning families
1. **Track selection** — choose which geometry/targets the tool intersects.
2. **Persistent periodic action** — exploit repeated tool behavior while player acts independently.
3. **Catch-position planning** — launch state determines where the player should be later.
4. **Anchor hand-off** — physically transfer the same tool between orbit systems.
5. **Phase/ordering** — release/catch sequence controls when periodic effects occur, without pixel timing.
6. **Interception/coverage** — orbiting tool temporarily blocks or intersects a periodic hazard.
7. **Return inversion/state mutation** — changed geometry makes an old orbit wrong and forces reassignment.

## 4–8 hour campaign burden
Plausible only if the game resists tool/target inflation.

Preferred product ceiling for tournament pressure:
- one iconic baseline tool;
- 2–3 orbit track shapes per anchor family;
- 3 reusable target/hazard relationships;
- 25–30 main encounters rather than padding to prove value.

If mature depth requires five specialized tools or many switch types, the concept loses its clean second-actor identity.

## Destructive attack
**Dominant strategy:** throw tool onto whichever track visibly hits the required switch and wait.

Defense requires player movement/catching/transfer to be completion-critical in mature content. A room solvable by parking the wrench indefinitely is teaching content only.

**Waiting:** periodicity can accidentally produce idle play. Normal solution traces should use the orbit as a moving window while the player traverses, not ask the player to watch cycles.

**Execution burden:** the biggest risk. If missing a catch or track entry is common after the player understands the solution, action friction overwhelms puzzle reasoning.

**Market abstraction:** broad `orbit game` language is weak because current orbit/tether games are active. Store presentation must show the tool as a second actor and the player moving/catching independently, not sell realistic orbital mechanics.

**Hour-5 repetition:** `orbit hits switch` is dangerous. Hand-off, interception, catch-position and state mutation must remain central without adding a second system.

## Accessibility/readability
Controller-native if anchors magnetically capture in a clear zone and tracks are previewed. No analog ballistic aiming should be required. Reduced-motion cannot remove the tool's actual periodic motion, but motion trail/ghost path can be simplified; simulation-speed assist may be a future empirical accessibility option if it preserves ordering.

## One-week kill prototype
Graybox:
- one player capsule;
- one wrench;
- two anchors with inner/outer authored tracks;
- two switches;
- one simple periodic hazard;
- one objective mutation;
- controller support from day one.

**PASS if:**
- unfamiliar players intentionally select a track after one teaching room;
- successful catches exceed roughly 85–90% once the player is in the correct planned region;
- players naturally move while the tool orbits rather than stand and watch;
- a mature test requires launch -> independent traversal -> catch/transfer, not only `hit switch`;
- players describe the tool as something they are coordinating with, not a projectile they are aiming.

**FAIL if:**
- catch/launch misses dominate failures;
- the game needs realistic momentum/orbit equations to feel interesting;
- players park the tool and wait through cycles;
- mature problems are mostly variations of `which circle hits the button?`.

# 5. G5C17 — Door Memory

## Frozen tournament pitch
**Every special doorway remembers HEAVY or LIGHT, and any eligible object crossing swaps its weight state with the door, turning route order into a physical memory puzzle.**

## Exact smallest vocabulary
- top-down/small-room traversal;
- eligible objects have binary state HEAVY/LIGHT;
- each memory door stores exactly the same binary state;
- when an eligible object crosses, **door and object swap states atomically**;
- HEAVY/LIGHT affects only a bounded visible consequence set such as pushability and weight-triggered mechanisms;
- no second property family in the tournament product;
- no direct property-edit cursor, inventory of traits, logic editor or arbitrary object metadata.

## Normal-play 10-second mute trailer trace
0–2s: a visibly HEAVY crate is pushed toward a visibly LIGHT doorway.
2–4s: crate crosses; doorway motif flips to HEAVY and crate visibly becomes LIGHT/easy to move.
4–6s: player quickly pushes the now-light crate aside, exposing a small cart.
6–8s: cart crosses the now-HEAVY doorway; doorway flips LIGHT, cart lands heavily on a weight plate.
8–10s: plate activates a mechanism and the player moves through.

The mechanic can be shown, but it needs more symbolic literacy than Tension/Orbit.

## 20-minute demo paper trace
**0–5 min — One door / one object.** Crossing swaps visible state; player learns the rule.

**5–9 min — State priming.** Player chooses which object should cross first so the next crossing receives the useful state.

**9–14 min — Two doors.** Local door memories create a sequencing problem rather than a global property pool.

**14–18 min — Capability coupling.** HEAVY/LIGHT affects movement/plate/lift access; player plans order and physical route.

**18–20 min — Memory poison / extraction.** Solving the objective leaves the wrong stored state for return; player must repair the sequence.

## Distinct mature reasoning families
1. **Door-state priming** — stage the memory for a later object.
2. **Carrier ordering** — object A crosses mainly to configure object B.
3. **Two-door state transport** — route order moves the one-bit state through space.
4. **Capability conversion** — light state enables movement; heavy state enables mechanisms.
5. **Memory poisoning** — a locally correct crossing leaves a later harmful state.
6. **Return inversion** — extraction needs the door memory restored/reversed.

The families are legitimate, but several are variants of ordered binary-state transport.

## 4–8 hour campaign burden
Technically cheap, but content ceiling is the concern. A mostly one-bit campaign can likely support a compact 3–5 hour puzzle game; stretching toward 8 hours risks either box-hauling friction or adding properties, which is explicitly disallowed for tournament comparison.

## Destructive attack
**Dominant strategy:** use one convenient object repeatedly as a state shuttle, turning puzzles into hauling/reset work.

**Brute force:** binary states make the state space small. Two doors + a few objects can be reasoned about, but weak rooms may be solved by trying crossing orders rather than understanding consequences.

**Execution:** low, but repeated crate transport may create dead movement.

**Tutorial burden:** moderate. The atomic swap must be perfectly represented; if players think the door merely `copies` the previous object's weight, failure becomes confusing.

**Current market pressure:** now significantly worse. `MODIFY` explicitly markets top-down puzzles around changing object weight, heavy/light behavior and SWAP operations. Door Memory is a narrower operator, but the product's most attractive examples sit inside the competitor's current property-edit language.

## Accessibility/readability
Strong controller viability and low execution demand. Requires shape/icon redundancy for HEAVY/LIGHT states and an unmistakable two-way swap animation. Color alone is insufficient.

## One-week kill prototype
Three tiny rooms with:
- two memory doors;
- three eligible objects;
- HEAVY/LIGHT only;
- plate + pushability consequences;
- no text after first teaching card.

**PASS if:** players can predict door/object states after crossing with very low error and solve a two-door room without external state notes.

**FAIL if:** players confuse copy vs swap; optimal play becomes repetitive shuttle hauling; three mature rooms already feel like permutations of the same binary sequence; or store-position testing shows players describe it primarily as `another property-swap puzzle`.

# 6. G5C21 — Broken Rule Workshop

## Frozen tournament pitch
**A live machine has exactly one wrong physical rule cartridge: run it, watch the deterministic symptom chain, infer the causal fault, swap one cartridge, and run it again.**

## Exact smallest vocabulary
- one small visible machine per encounter built from reusable physical modules;
- machine has one deterministic input/output goal;
- exactly one rule cartridge is wrong at baseline;
- cartridges are chunky world objects in visible sockets, not wire/code text;
- tournament vocabulary uses four rule families maximum: **DIRECTION, ORDER, CONDITION, DELAY**;
- player can inspect/remove/replace one cartridge and press RUN/RESET;
- run is short enough that observation/revision repeats every ~5–15 seconds;
- no free circuit drawing, code entry, factory construction or large inventory.

## Normal-play 10-second mute trailer trace
0–3s: player presses RUN; a clamp closes late, press fires first, object shoots sideways/jams. Cause chain is visually obvious.
3–5s: player pulls one bright ORDER cartridge from a physical socket.
5–7s: snaps the alternate cartridge into the same slot and hits RUN.
7–10s: clamp now closes before press; object is processed cleanly and lands in the output tray.

Strong clip if machine motion dominates the frame; weak if the cartridge rack dominates.

## 20-minute demo paper trace
**0–4 min — Direction fault.** One obvious wrong cartridge, immediate rerun.

**4–8 min — Order fault.** Two modules each work, but causal order is wrong. First real diagnosis.

**8–12 min — Condition fault.** Machine behaves correctly in one branch and incorrectly in another, forcing observation rather than visual inspection alone.

**12–16 min — Delay fault.** Symptom appears downstream; player must reason backward through visible sequence.

**16–20 min — Dual symptom / one cause.** Two visible failures share one upstream cartridge; final repair proves diagnosis rather than patching each symptom.

## Distinct mature reasoning families
1. **Direct localization** — one visible wrong behavior maps near the faulty rule.
2. **Causal ordering** — correct parts execute in wrong sequence.
3. **Conditional inversion** — branch behaves under the wrong sensor condition.
4. **Temporal diagnosis** — downstream symptom comes from an upstream delay.
5. **Shared-cause diagnosis** — multiple symptoms trace to one rule.
6. **Counterfactual testing** — choose the replacement whose predicted run separates two plausible diagnoses.
7. **Alternative valid repair / mastery** — later optional goals may distinguish functional vs efficient repair without new core mechanics.

## 4–8 hour campaign burden
This is the candidate's largest cost. Even with reusable modules, each good machine needs:
- readable baseline purpose;
- a short visually legible causal chain;
- one interesting wrong rule;
- meaningful decoy hypotheses without hidden information;
- animation that makes the symptom observable.

A 4–6 hour product may need roughly 25–35 carefully authored machines. That is more bespoke design/animation burden than Tension or Orbit, even if code reuse is high.

## Destructive attack
**Brute force:** swap cartridges and rerun until success. Because one wrong slot and a small replacement set are intentionally simple, blind search can be cheap.

Defense cannot be a punitive run timer. Mature machines need enough plausible candidate faults that observation substantially reduces attempts; mastery can reward first-try diagnosis but main completion should still tolerate experimentation.

**UI/editor drift:** fatal if the player views a detached wiring board or reads boolean expressions. The machine must remain the primary information surface.

**Waiting:** run loops must be short; no 30-second machine cycle.

**Content repetition:** four rule families may eventually feel like textbook `spot the wrong logic`. Physical module behavior and causal topology must create variety without adding a fifth/sixth rule family every chapter.

**Market pressure:** `The Signal State` and `Linkito` strongly occupy machine-repair/logic-block territory, but they emphasize construction/manipulation of networks. The one-wrong-rule observational-diagnosis loop is narrower enough to survive this pass.

## Accessibility/readability
Controller can use local socket targeting and a small physical cartridge rack. No tiny wires/cursor placement. Machine speed should support pause/replay/slow observation without changing rule truth. Rule families need icon/shape/text redundancy and a high-contrast causal-run mode.

## One-week kill prototype
Build three machines from the same 6–8 reusable modules:
- one ORDER fault;
- one CONDITION fault;
- one DELAY/shared-symptom fault;
- 2–4 plausible cartridge alternatives per machine;
- no detached logic view.

**PASS if:**
- players explain the visible causal reason for at least two repairs rather than saying they guessed;
- median reruns trend down as understanding improves;
- machine observation, not cartridge browsing, occupies most attention/time;
- three distinct machines can be authored from reused modules without one-off scripting.

**FAIL if:**
- brute swapping is consistently faster than diagnosis;
- players ask for a circuit/wiring overview to understand state;
- each new puzzle needs custom module behavior/animation;
- the machine run is passive/boring after the first few attempts.

# 8. Run-1 tournament elimination record

## Eliminated: G5C17 Door Memory
The elimination is not based on low technical quality. It is based on the combination of:
1. new August-2026 `MODIFY` pressure directly using weight/heavy/light/property SWAP language in a minimalist top-down puzzle;
2. a 4–8h depth ceiling that is strongest only while the product remains one-bit;
3. rescue through additional property families would make market collision and explanation burden worse;
4. repeated physical state shuttling risks becoming box-hauling friction;
5. the remaining three entrants offer stronger product distance or broader systemic/experiential upside.

Door Memory remains historical research only. Do not revive it during this tournament unless materially new evidence changes the collision/depth diagnosis.
