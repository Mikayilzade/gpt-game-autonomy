## 2. Equal destructive probes

### A — Margin of Error — SURVIVE
**Why scores hold.** The visible promise is unusual and immediate: the ruler itself is wrong. The game can expose exact interval bounds graphically, so failure can be certified rather than hand-waved. Depth comes from correlated error, datum choice, tolerance stack-up, cancellation, go/no-go fixtures and designing for worst-case rather than discovering one magic number.

**Tutorial case.** Two nominally 40 mm blocks must span a socket. Ruler A is disclosed as +2 mm biased. Player may mark both cuts from the same datum or independently. One approach causes error to compound; common referencing makes the relationship safe. Final physical fit test explains the result.

**Late case.** Build a three-link tabletop latch where two rulers have different disclosed interval errors, one stock face is the only trustworthy datum, a purchased pin has its own tolerance, and the latch must work at both extreme assemblies. Same verbs: choose datum, measure/mark, cut/position, mate, certify.

**Dominant strategy.** Always use the widest safety clearance. Defeat it with paired min/max functional constraints: too loose fails backlash/alignment while too tight jams. No score chasing required.

**Meaningful branching.** Datum/reference choice changes which errors correlate; cut order changes what can still be referenced. Branching is structural, not thousands of arbitrary lengths.

**Learning after failure.** The certifier shows which worst-case endpoint failed and which tolerance chain produced it, never the winning action sequence.

**Attempt #20 freshness.** Different correlation structures and functional envelopes, not merely smaller tolerances.

**Ugliest risk.** Accidentally teaching formal tolerance analysis through spreadsheet-like UI. Round B must prove a tactile visual interval language requiring little arithmetic.

### B — Second-Hand Forecast — KILL
**Tutorial.** Two instruments: moving a fictional hygrometer beside a warm housing biases it by +1 band; isolate it with a baffle and choose tomorrow's icon.
**Late.** Five devices with pairwise heat/air couplings, two possible baffle positions and a required forecast under two environmental hypotheses.
**Dominant strategy.** Isolate every device. Constraints can limit baffles, but that turns the game into resource allocation around hidden causal rules.
**Branching.** Device placement × baffles × inferred environment; much of it is epistemic rather than visibly mechanical.
**Learning.** A failed forecast can reveal which reading was contaminated, but unless all couplings are pre-disclosed the player learns designer-authored exceptions.
**Attempt #20.** New instruments/couplings—content burden rather than clean recombination.
**Ugliest risk.** Opaque causality plus text-heavy forecasting fiction.
**Kill gate.** Explainable deterministic failure is too weak. Making all couplings explicit removes the diagnosis fantasy; hiding them creates trial-and-error. KILL.

### C — The Spare Room — SURVIVE
**Tutorial.** Place bed, desk and chair in a 5x5 room. Press a lever to preview the disclosed alternate wall/door positions. Both states require bed-side access and chair pull-out zone.
**Late.** Four objects with asymmetric use zones, a hinged wardrobe clearance, two doors and two disclosed floorplans sharing only part of their cells. Same verbs: select, rotate, socket to grid, inspect both states, certify.
**Dominant strategy.** Put everything in the geometric intersection of both rooms. Defeat via required use zones/door access and objects whose legal regions are intentionally complementary.
**Branching.** Position/orientation choices alter overlapping access zones in both states.
**Learning.** Failure highlights the violated use zone in state A or B and the blocking object.
**Attempt #20.** New paired-state relationships and asymmetric use footprints; no new verbs needed.
**Ugliest risk.** Becoming generic Sokoban/packing with two boards. Round B must prove that paired-state reasoning changes solutions qualitatively.

### D — One-Way Workshop — SURVIVE
**Tutorial.** One stock strip, two discrete cut sockets. Cut the needed rail; the offcut becomes the only legal spacer to position a second hole/cut. Player sees both outputs before committing.
**Late.** A blank offers four legal cut lines over a 5-step job. Offcuts can serve only by typed properties (straight-edge length band, spacer width, angle template), and later operations consume or occupy them. Same verbs: inspect plan, choose discrete cut, assign resulting part/offcut as jig, perform next operation, certify assembly.
**Dominant strategy.** Preserve the largest offcut. Defeat by jobs requiring incompatible jig properties and by cuts where a smaller offcut has the necessary dimension/type.
**Branching.** Each cut deterministically creates two typed future resources; only a small legal cut vocabulary avoids combinatorial explosion.
**Learning.** Failure states exactly which downstream operation lacks a valid jig and traces the offcut lineage without revealing the correct first cut.
**Attempt #20.** Different dependency graphs between product parts and tool-like byproducts.
**Ugliest risk.** If cuts become continuous/freeform, authoring/certification explodes. Round B must prove a discrete cut grammar still feels like fabrication rather than menu selection.

### E — Unpacking Order — SURVIVE
**Tutorial.** Pack three rigid objects in a small case. Extraction order is wrench → bottle → book; each object has 1–2 allowed pull vectors. Player packs, closes case, then watches/executes the requested removals without moving non-target objects.
**Late.** Seven objects, asymmetric shapes, side/top extraction vectors, one handle-clearance requirement and an order containing two interchangeable middle targets. Same verbs: place/rotate into sockets, inspect vector clearance, seal, extract in requested order.
**Dominant strategy.** Reverse-pack the requested extraction order. That is useful intuition but insufficient when objects have incompatible insertion/extraction vectors, side apertures, shared clearance corridors and partial-order rather than total-order demands.
**Branching.** Packing creates a directed blocking graph; meaningful alternatives are different graph structures, not tiny coordinate perturbations.
**Learning.** Failed extraction shows the blocked vector and blocker; player can reason backward.
**Attempt #20.** New target dependency graphs, aperture topology and object vector sets.
**Ugliest risk.** 3D occlusion/readability and accidentally becoming a generic packing game. Round B must formalize discrete geometry and prove reverse-order packing is not universal.

### F — Quiet Please — KILL
**Tutorial.** Two clockwork appliances with 4-step cycles; choose one of two phase sockets so loud ticks differ.
**Late.** Five cycles across two acoustic zones with shared drive phase.
**Dominant strategy.** Spread peaks uniformly; richer harmonics can defeat it.
**Branching.** Phase/socket assignments.
**Learning.** Timeline clearly shows collision.
**Attempt #20.** More periods/zones/harmonics.
**Ugliest risk.** The exact intellectual heart is cross-cycle phase composition.
**Kill gate.** This is materially adjacent to #011 Missing Step's cyclic-program period/phase relationships. Presentation differences do not create enough portfolio distance. KILL regardless of otherwise healthy score.

### G — Borrowed Floor — KILL
**Tutorial.** Place a shelf on two temporary props, insert final legs, remove props in a legal order.
**Late.** A display of four final pieces and three props where removability and support dependencies cross.
**Dominant strategy.** Replace temporary support immediately with permanent support. Could be defeated by access blocking.
**Branching.** Assembly/support order.
**Learning.** Unsupported object/blocked prop can be highlighted.
**Attempt #20.** Different support/access graphs.
**Ugliest risk.** Players bring continuous real-world gravity/stability expectations; a discrete support graph will often contradict the visual scene, while real physics makes failure noisy.
**Kill gate.** The concept either becomes precision/physics-dependent or requires conspicuously artificial support semantics. KILL.

### H — Lost Motion — SURVIVE
**Tutorial.** A crank should move a bell only on the return stroke. Insert one of three discrete backlash collars; the correct deadzone absorbs forward motion and engages on reversal.
**Late.** Three linked rotary/linear elements with discrete slack bands; requirements specify output windows on direction changes, including one intentional missed stroke. Same verbs: choose linkage socket, choose backlash element/orientation, crank through a disclosed finite input trace, inspect output trace, certify.
**Dominant strategy.** Add maximum slack. Defeat with upper/lower engagement windows: too much slack misses required output.
**Branching.** Slack placement and orientation changes where motion is absorbed; finite trace enables proof.
**Learning.** Overlay input travel, absorbed travel and transmitted travel so a failure has a mechanical cause.
**Attempt #20.** Direction reversals, stacked deadzones, asymmetric slack and output-window patterns.
**Ugliest risk.** Animation/UI must make an invisible interval feel physical. Round B needs a minimal deterministic backlash algebra and a legible 10-second clip.

### I — The Long Drawer — SURVIVE
**Tutorial.** Three drawers share one depth lane. Extending A reveals a side handle on B; B at half extension blocks C. Retrieve tool C then tool A.
**Late.** Five drawers with 3–4 discrete extension states, side apertures and exposed latch/handle predicates. Request is a short retrieval sequence, and closing a drawer may hide a control needed later. Same verbs: pull/push one discrete stop, use newly reachable handle/latch, retrieve target.
**Dominant strategy.** Fully open everything. Shared depth lanes and handle occlusion make full extension actively block alternatives.
**Branching.** Only actions whose handles are currently reachable; designed state graph has multiple plausible routes and reversible mistakes.
**Learning.** Blocked action visually shows collision or inaccessible handle, not an abstract error.
**Attempt #20.** New cabinet topology, access-from-side relationships and target sequences.
**Ugliest risk.** Finite-state blind search. Round B must prove information-rich states and deduction shortcuts, plus prevent BFS-by-clicking from being the easiest human strategy.

### J — Phase Change — KILL
**Tutorial.** Heat one wax-like tile so it crosses from insulating to conducting, powering a sink one turn later.
**Late.** Two sources, three material types, thermal bands and a route that must conduct then self-break after cooling.
**Dominant strategy.** Heat everything. Upper temperature limits and cooling could defeat it.
**Branching.** Placement + turn-by-turn material bands.
**Learning.** Heat arrows/band transitions can explain local changes.
**Attempt #20.** Different thermal topology and transition thresholds.
**Ugliest risk.** Simultaneous-vs-sequential update order, propagation previews, and cascading band changes demand a simulation UI before the puzzle becomes intuitive.
**Kill gate.** Not impossible, but production/readability cost is disproportionate to its relatively familiar circuit/heat-routing surface. KILL in this field.

### K — Please Hold — KILL
**Tutorial.** Three calls, one operator, physical loops of 1/2/3 turns; park one call so it returns after the operator finishes another.
**Late.** Six calls with visible service windows, shared loops and deliberate missed returns.
**Dominant strategy.** Assign delay equal to next free operator slot. Resource contention complicates it but reinforces scheduling.
**Branching.** Call-to-loop assignment over turns.
**Learning.** Timeline makes missed windows clear.
**Attempt #20.** New arrival/window patterns and loop sharing.
**Ugliest risk.** Despite tactile cables, the solved object is a scheduling table. The physical loop becomes a skin over integer delays.
**Kill gate.** Core pleasure is insufficiently physical and risks finite scheduling enumeration. KILL.

### L — Pressure Proof — KILL
**Tutorial.** A soft block is one band too wide; choose a gauge whose test compresses one axis and then see whether it fits a final socket.
**Late.** Three test fixtures reveal/alter different shape bands; final part must meet two contact predicates after certification.
**Dominant strategy.** Avoid optional tests. Can force uncertainty, but then information acquisition becomes state-changing history.
**Branching.** Test order and fixture choice.
**Learning.** Each deformation is deterministic and visible.
**Attempt #20.** New deformation grammars/fixture sequences.
**Ugliest risk.** The conceptual signature is persistent state-changing tests whose order/history matters.
**Kill gate.** Too close to #013 irreversible evidence/testing and #015 persistent transformation histories. A new material skin is not enough distance. KILL.
