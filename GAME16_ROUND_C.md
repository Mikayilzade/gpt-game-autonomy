# 1. One-Way Workshop (OWW)

**Store hook:** Every cut gives you the part you wanted—and an offcut you must turn into the tool that makes a later operation possible.

**10-second trailer:** Player commits a bright socketed cut through a wooden blank. Two children separate. The apparently useless triangular offcut is rotated, snapped into a jig station, and instantly becomes the angle guide for drilling/cutting the final product; cut lineage glows from waste → tool → finished assembly.

**First 15 minutes:** 0–3 min: inspect one plan; cut a strip at one of two obvious sockets; offcut snaps as spacer; guided hole operation; certify. 3–7: job where largest offcut is wrong and a smaller width-band spacer is needed. 7–11: diagonal cut creates an angle template rather than a dimensional spacer. 11–15: first two-blank job: player must decide which blank supplies a future jig before making the visible product part. Canonical verbs only: inspect plan → choose stock/cut socket → commit → assign child as part/jig → perform jig-gated operation → assemble/certify.

**Midgame case:** two blanks, four operations. A short spacer is needed to locate holes on a rail; an angle template is needed for a brace; product ancestry and jig ancestry cross. Player sees all child properties before cutting, but must preserve the correct lineage.

**Late case:** three stock pieces, six irreversible operations, two valid solution families. One offcut can satisfy either a reference-edge jig or spacer station but cannot do both simultaneously; a second offcut becomes useful only after a guided operation adds a hole-pattern witness. No new primitive verb.

## 24-case architecture
Six reasoning families, four cases each:
1. **Immediate byproduct** (01–04): offcut directly gates next operation.
2. **Property choice** (05–08): length/width/reference-edge/angle properties compete.
3. **Delayed lineage** (09–12): preserve a child for a jig several steps later.
4. **Cross-blank ancestry** (13–16): product from blank A depends on jig born from B.
5. **Dual-use conflict** (17–20): one child fits multiple stations; occupation/consumption forces prioritization.
6. **Derived witness** (21–24): a child gains a typed witness only through a jig-gated operation, creating a second-order capability chain.

Late depth is dependency-graph depth, not new-rule inflation. Ceiling: 3 stock pieces, 6 committed fabrication operations, <=8 simultaneously loose children, tiny frozen property vocabulary.

**Demo promise:** ~20–30 min, cases 01–06 plus one curated cross-blank capstone. Ends on waste→tool→product chain that is visually obvious.

**Price/value hypothesis:** low-premium puzzle product, provisional $9.99–14.99 with $12.99 center pending later Phase-7 research. 24 dense authored cases can support that only if cases have multi-step reasoning and tactile presentation; do not inflate count with filler.

**Target player:** players who enjoy compact deterministic spatial/logic puzzles, physical craft toys, Zachtronics-like planning satisfaction without programming, and explainable irreversible choices.

**Strongest screenshot:** cut blank separated into two pieces, one product outline and one offcut visibly docked in a jig beside the partly assembled object; lineage marks connect them.
**Weakest screenshot:** static plan/menu with cut sockets and property icons; risks reading as CAD/educational UI.

**Risk ledger:** (R1) discrete sockets feel menu-like; (R2) lineage bookkeeping overwhelms tactile fantasy; (R3) property icons become abstract; (R4) workshop asset expectations expand; (R5) irreversible failure feels punitive. Mitigations: cuts are direct object manipulation; max loose-child ceiling; physical jig fit previews; tabletop diorama scope; instant restart/undo-before-commit boundary and precise failure ancestry.

**Smallest falsification prototype (future implementation gate, not factory code):** one blank mesh, 4 legal cut sockets, deterministic split children, two physical jig stations (spacer + angle template), one 4-operation job. It must prove controller selection feels like cutting rather than choosing a menu and that a player can explain why an offcut matters without reading numbers. Failure kills/reworks presentation, not the frozen causal thesis.

# 2. Unpacking Order (UO)

**Store hook:** Pack a case so every object can later be pulled out in a promised order—without moving anything else.

**10-second trailer:** case seals; request card flips to WRENCH FIRST; translucent side corridor appears; wrench slides cleanly through a narrow aperture while surrounding objects remain locked, then its removal opens the next corridor.

**First 15 minutes:** one top aperture/3 objects; vector overlay; seal-and-test; introduce side aperture; demonstrate why reverse-order packing is useful but not sufficient; first crossing swept corridor. Same verbs throughout.

**Midgame:** 5 objects, two apertures, first target can only leave sideways; later target occupies a top corridor that must be preserved.
**Late:** 7 objects, partial-order request, insertion/extraction aperture asymmetry and crossing swept corridors; no new verb.

## 24-case sketch / six families
01–04 single-aperture blockers; 05–08 vector asymmetry; 09–12 multiple apertures; 13–16 crossing corridors; 17–20 partial orders/interchangeable targets; 21–24 insertion-vs-extraction asymmetry compositions. Mechanically healthy, little rule inflation.

**Demo:** 6 cases, final seal→three-object extraction proof. **Price:** provisional $7.99–12.99 due direct comparison pressure from packing puzzle products. **Target:** spatial packing/logic players. **Strong screenshot:** sealed case plus glowing extraction corridor and numbered request. **Weak screenshot:** ordinary open suitcase full of objects—reads as generic packing.

**Risk/falsification:** 3D occlusion, pose selection, corridor readability, and market first-read. Smallest future prototype: 4 socketed objects, top+side aperture, translucent swept-volume inspection, seal/test. Must prove players understand a blocked vector immediately from controller distance.

# 3. Margin of Error (MoE)

**Store hook:** Build mechanisms with rulers that are knowingly wrong—make the errors cancel so every possible real part still fits.

**10-second trailer:** two marks made from the same battered ruler show broad uncertainty halos; parts snap together, halos cancel at the shared datum, and the physical latch passes both tight and loose certification extremes.

**First 15 minutes:** trusted datum; biased ruler; interval envelope shown physically; shared-reference cancellation; second job where too-wide clearance fails maximum-play bound; first independent-vs-correlated choice. No arithmetic entry.

**Midgame:** two error sources and purchased pin tolerance; choose which features share datum. **Late:** four features, mixed correlated/independent errors, min/max functional envelope, go/no-go gauge.

## 24-case sketch / six families
01–04 single bias; 05–08 shared-datum cancellation; 09–12 min/max fit envelopes; 13–16 independent-vs-correlated choice; 17–20 purchased-part tolerance; 21–24 mixed chains/go-no-go certification. No rule inflation, but representational complexity rises.

**Demo:** 5–6 bench builds ending with physical latch certification. **Price:** provisional $9.99–14.99. **Target:** logic/engineering-minded puzzle players, but must work for non-engineers. **Strong screenshot:** tactile assembly with visible uncertainty envelopes and final fit gauge. **Weak screenshot:** ruler, dimensions, interval bars—looks like coursework.

**Risk/falsification:** biggest risk is not formal mechanics but affect. Smallest future prototype: two blocks, one biased ruler, shared datum, physical interval halo and snap-fit reveal. A cold player must solve without arithmetic notation and describe the causal insight as physical rather than mathematical.

# 5. Pairwise confrontation

## OWW vs UO
- Fun before difficulty: tie/OWW slight win—cut/split/snap jig is inherently satisfying; UO place/rotate is familiar.
- Failure explanation: UO slight win—blocked corridor is immediate; OWW needs lineage UI.
- Hour-10 depth: OWW win—capability ancestry and dual-use byproducts support deeper planning without object-shape escalation.
- Market readability: OWW win—packing imagery is crowded in 2026.
- Production ratio: OWW slight win if discrete; UO requires robust 3D swept-volume visualization and many readable object shapes.
- Controller/Deck: UO slight win; OWW remains viable with socketed cuts.
- Portfolio value: OWW win—strong new causal identity.
**Why UO loses:** it is mechanically excellent, but its differentiator arrives after the market first reads “packing game.” OWW's defining event is visible in one cut and has a stronger novel causal sentence.

## OWW vs MoE
- Fun before difficulty: OWW clear win—physical cut/byproduct/jig interaction needs less conceptual explanation.
- Failure explanation: tie after ancestry tracing vs worst-case endpoint trace.
- Hour-10 depth: tie; both have strong compositional reasoning.
- Market readability: OWW win; MoE risks educational/engineering perception.
- Production ratio: MoE code/asset ratio may be excellent, but UI pedagogy risk is high; OWW wins overall product ratio.
- Controller/Deck: OWW slight win due direct sockets; MoE interval inspection can become cursor/UI dense.
- Portfolio value: tie/high.
**Why MoE loses:** its formal system may be the strongest, but the product must first persuade non-engineers that tolerance propagation is play. OWW begins with a pleasurable irreversible physical act and reveals the reasoning through consequences.

## UO vs MoE
UO wins immediate physicality, tutorial ease and failure visibility; MoE wins differentiation, anti-bruteforce proof and formal depth. UO's crowded surface and MoE's homework surface are both material commercial risks. Neither beats OWW's combination of causal novelty and tactile legibility.

# 6. Winner
**GAME #016 WINNER: ONE-WAY WORKSHOP.**

The selection is not score arithmetic. OWW uniquely combines: a one-sentence causal hook; a 10-second irreversible transformation; deterministic proof; a stable tiny verb set; depth from dependency ancestry; bounded authored content; strong demo climax; and market identity that does not depend on a crowded genre label.

Unpacking Order is rejected for Game #016 because market first-read and 3D packing imagery dilute its otherwise excellent extraction mechanic. Margin of Error is rejected because the presentation/pedagogy burden stands between the player and the fun earlier than OWW's risk does.

## Empirical gates carried forward
1. Discrete cut sockets must feel like manipulating a workpiece, not selecting menu options.
2. Offcut lineage must remain readable at 6 operations / <=8 loose children.
3. A cold player must understand typed jig compatibility mostly from shape/fit, not text/property arithmetic.
4. Irreversible commits must create tension without restart friction.
5. Workshop presentation must remain tabletop/bounded; do not drift into woodworking simulation or shop management.
