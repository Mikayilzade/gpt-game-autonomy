## 2. Fresh market scan — September 2026
Research performed 2026-09-02.

Sources consulted:
- Steam Next Fest June 2026 coverage: Thinky Third Thursday, GamesRadar, GameSpot, Crit Pick, Gamedō/Backlog Coach coverage.
- 2026 puzzle context: `Order of the Sinking Star`, `Screenbound`, `Demon Bluff`, `AntiMatcher`, `Colorbound`, `Gridwalker`, `Woodo`, `TOEM 2`, `Beyond Words` and adjacent experimental indies.
- Gamescom/autumn 2026 coverage for broader attention competition.

### Market conclusions
1. **Puzzle supply is not scarce; instantly legible differentiation is.** June Next Fest exposed thousands of demos, so a competent abstract puzzler without a GIF-readable hook is commercially fragile.
2. **Sokoban lineage is especially dangerous for differentiation in 2026.** `Order of the Sinking Star` arrives with 1,000+ authored puzzles and several interacting puzzle systems; Game #014 should not compete on quantity or generic push-grid cleverness.
3. **Rule discovery remains attractive, but presentation must carry the premise.** AntiMatcher/Colorbound/Gridwalker-style coverage shows appetite for concise strange rules, while Screenbound demonstrates the marketing power of a mechanic visible immediately in motion.
4. **Roguelike/deckbuilder fusion is crowded.** Beyond Words, RACCOIN, Shroom and Gloom and many adjacent releases reinforce that adding cards/random runs is not differentiation by itself.
5. **Demo quality and immediate comprehension matter disproportionately.** With several thousand festival demos, the design should produce a complete satisfying 15–30 minute slice and a trailer beat within seconds.
6. **Short premium can be viable if the idea is clean.** A 2–5 hour authored game is acceptable; do not inflate content toward a 1,000-puzzle arms race.
7. **Portfolio implication:** seek a physical/semantic rule that is visible, deterministic, controller-friendly, cheap to author, and yields inverse reasoning or constraint interaction without becoming bookkeeping.

## 3. Phase-1 scoring model
Each surviving concept will later be attacked on 1–5 scores for:
- H: one-sentence / one-GIF hook clarity
- D: depth from interacting rules rather than content count
- R: low repetition risk after 2–4 hours
- S: solo implementation/content scope
- M: market differentiation in 2026
- X: portfolio distance from #001–#013
- V: demo/trailer strength

Phase 1 does not pretend these scores prove a winner. Any concept with a fatal collision, generic core, expensive content dependency, or depth-by-parameter-inflation is killed before tournament.

## 4. Broad field — 30 concepts

1. **Negative Casting** — place opaque objects between two lamps; the puzzle judges only the *overlap and subtraction of their cast shadows* on several walls. Risk: could collapse into spatial trial-and-error.
2. **Museum of Wrong Labels** — exhibits obey their physical rules, but movable museum labels redefine which rule-name applies to which visible behavior; infer and place the vocabulary correctly. Risk: semantic gimmick may become matching exercise.
3. **Afterimage Clerk** — every moved object leaves one temporary solid afterimage at its previous location; solve by deliberately sequencing which past positions remain obstacles/tools. Risk: collision with generic temporal Sokoban if movement dominates.
4. **Counterfeit Window** — assemble a stained-glass window where pieces are judged differently from front/back because lead lines occlude color boundaries; satisfy paired silhouettes without folding. Risk: packing/search burden.
5. **The Missing Reflection** — arrange a tiny room so mirrors collectively show a required set of objects while specified objects must be absent from every reflection. Risk: 3D camera ambiguity and ray bookkeeping.
6. **Borrowed Horizon** — rotate small landscape layers; each layer changes what counts as skyline for layers behind it, and clues constrain only visible peaks/valleys. Risk: may become arithmetic height comparison.
7. **Quiet Assembly** — connect machine parts, but every joint transmits a named vibration category; goal is to make required outputs move while forbidden sensors remain silent. Risk: network propagation may resemble #005.
8. **One-Way Museum** — place doors in a floorplan; each door permanently changes which side of a room is considered its public entrance, changing exhibit visibility constraints. Risk: adjacency/topology collision with #006.
9. **Ink Debt** — stamp overlapping transparent marks onto a form; later stamps erase exactly the ink they overlap rather than adding it, so final surviving glyph fragments encode approval conditions. Risk: visual combinatorics could be opaque.
10. **False Bottom** — pack objects into shallow drawers where only the topmost visible cells count toward inspection, but hidden support cells determine which top cells can legally remain. Risk: packing genre saturation.
11. **Second Hand** — set several analog clock hands, then choose one global elapsed interval; each clock has different stopped/slipping hand rules inferred from evidence, and final relationships—not absolute times—are judged. Risk: arithmetic/time-zone feel.
12. **Receipt Reconstruction** — reconstruct a shopping receipt from torn totals where tax/discount lines have positional scope; infer line ordering from subtotal fragments without doing large arithmetic. Risk: spreadsheet aesthetic and arithmetic burden.
13. **Blind Carbon** — type once through a stack of carbon-paper forms with cut-outs; each sheet receives a different subset of strokes, and all resulting forms must satisfy distinct templates. Risk: visually adjacent to paper portfolio #009.
14. **The Unsent Letter** — arrange envelope windows and a single printed sheet so different folds-free translations expose different recipient/message fragments under multiple envelopes. Risk: geometric search and paper-theme collision.
15. **Pressure Proof** — press a few stamps into layered soft material; a deeper stamp destroys shallower impressions where they overlap, and clues concern surviving depth contours. Risk: may echo #013 irreversible evidence.
16. **Shared Alibi** — schedule a tiny cast across rooms, but clues refer only to pairs who could *not* have seen one another because of facing/occlusion; reconstruct a valid tableau, no dialogue tree. Risk: CSP may feel like generic logic-grid puzzle.
17. **Conservation of Lies** — each witness statement contains exactly one word whose category is wrong; moving category headers changes which word is the lie, and all statements must become simultaneously self-consistent. Risk: localization-heavy semantic authoring.
18. **Redaction Engine** — place black redaction bars over one document; visible fragments must simultaneously support several requested readings, with bars allowed to split words but never reorder text. Risk: language dependence and content-authoring cost.
19. **Proofreader's Ghost** — corrections are applied in a fixed visible order; deleting one correction changes the text positions targeted by all later corrections. Predict the final text from a short edit script. Risk: can become program-execution bookkeeping.
20. **Casting Call** — actors stand behind nested stage flats; move only the flats, not actors, so each audience seat sees an exact required cast subset. Risk: line-of-sight search may be too brute-force.
21. **Unreliable Scale** — balance several objects on scales whose zero marks and direction are wrong in different known ways; infer relative mass ordering using only comparison outcomes, then construct a target chain. Risk: familiar balance puzzle and numeric abstraction.
22. **Archive Compression** — stack transparent archival cards; identical symbols cancel in pairs while unique symbols survive, so order matters only through occlusion masks, not arithmetic. Risk: XOR-like abstraction may be hard to market.
23. **Palette Witness** — colored lights illuminate neutral sculptures; shadows remove individual light channels, so each surface can reveal mixed colors that encode which occluders intervene. Risk: color accessibility and 3D complexity.
24. **Cutaway Apartment** — choose which walls of a dollhouse become cutaway; removing a wall reveals rooms but also exposes objects that violate privacy constraints. Goal is a required information set with minimum exposure. Risk: optimization rather than deduction.
25. **Misprint Press** — build a short printing sequence from plates whose ink transfers only onto currently blank paper; later plates cannot overwrite existing ink. Reconstruct plate order/rotation from final multi-color print. Risk: order enumeration if deductions are weak.
26. **Stage Directions** — place a few directional spotlights; actors cast shadows that count as stage regions, and each actor's instruction references whether another actor is lit, silhouetted, or hidden from a named seat. Risk: predicate explosion.
27. **Parallax Filing** — align several perforated cards at different depths so one set of marks is visible from three fixed inspection angles; no folding, only translation. Risk: could become tedious geometric alignment.
28. **Edge of Evidence** — reconstruct a broken ceramic plate from fragments, but scoring ignores image matching and instead uses which painted lines terminate at a break versus continue across joins. Risk: jigsaw perception may dominate logic.
29. **Dry Run** — route a single pulse through a board where components react only on their first activation; repeated visits pass through inertly, so route order changes later connectivity without deleting components. Risk: route-planning collision and state bookkeeping.
30. **Curtain Ledger** — stack movable theater curtains with windows; each cue opens/closes one curtain, and clues concern which props were visible to which audience zones across a short cue sequence. Risk: temporal evidence could collide with #013.

## 6. Retained Phase-2 field — 12

### A. Negative Casting (#1)
Hook: construct *negative space made by intersecting shadows*, not the objects themselves.
Why retained: exceptional GIF readability; cheap geometry/content; potential inverse reasoning across multiple projection planes.
Failure hypothesis: once players learn silhouette overlap, puzzles become rotational trial-and-error with no new deductions.
Phase-2 attack: enumerate small 2-light/2-wall instances and identify at least 4 qualitatively different deductions before search.

### B. Museum of Wrong Labels (#2)
Hook: physical exhibits stay fixed; you move the names of the laws until every observed behavior and caption is simultaneously true.
Why retained: semantic/physical hybrid unlike portfolio; strong theme; can teach concepts through play.
Failure hypothesis: degenerates into permutation matching where each exhibit independently identifies one label.
Attack: require coupled ambiguity where no exhibit is individually identifiable and prove multi-clue deductions.

### C. Counterfeit Window (#4)
Hook: build one stained-glass object that passes different front/back silhouette inspections because lead occludes boundaries asymmetrically.
Why retained: tangible, visual, compact.
Failure hypothesis: becomes generic polyomino packing with cosmetic inspection rules.
Attack: prototype abstract cell model and prove front/back constraints create deductions unavailable to ordinary packing.

### D. The Missing Reflection (#5)
Hook: satisfy what several mirrors must show—and what none are allowed to show.
Why retained: instantly legible negative-information fantasy; strong trailer moments.
Failure hypothesis: ray tracing creates fiddly camera geometry and brute-force placement rather than reasoning.
Attack: constrain to discrete 2D top-down mirror optics first; test uniqueness and human deductions without continuous angles.

### E. Ink Debt (#9)
Hook: every new stamp *erases* overlapping old ink; author the final surviving fragments by choosing stamp order and placement.
Why retained: visually satisfying destructive composition without history-as-evidence; simple deterministic rule.
Failure hypothesis: order enumeration dominates and resembles generic layering puzzles.
Attack: search tiny instances for survivorship deductions, forced-last/forced-first logic, and nontrivial equivalent-order classes.

### F. False Bottom (#10)
Hook: inspection sees only top surfaces, while hidden supports decide which visible arrangement is physically legal.
Why retained: two-layer truth/appearance hook; compact content.
Failure hypothesis: ordinary packing dominates and hidden support becomes a secondary constraint.
Attack: eliminate freeform packing; test fixed slots/support footprints where deductions couple visible and hidden layers.

### G. Shared Alibi (#16)
Hook: reconstruct a room tableau from statements about who *could not have seen whom*.
Why retained: human-readable occlusion logic; no dialogue tree; strong mystery framing without generic clue grid.
Failure hypothesis: ultimately a standard CSP with decorative sight lines.
Attack: require viewpoint/occluder deductions that cannot be expressed as simple pairwise adjacency/exclusion.

### H. Casting Call (#20)
Hook: actors never move; slide stage flats until each audience seat sees exactly its required cast.
Why retained: one-screen theatrical presentation, inverse visibility reasoning, controller-friendly discrete state.
Failure hypothesis: exhaustive line-of-sight search is easier than deduction and content families repeat.
Attack: enumerate discrete flats/seats and prove blocker-sharing, interval and forced-visibility deductions.

### I. Palette Witness (#23)
Hook: shadows selectively remove RGB light channels, so a sculpture's colors reveal the hidden occlusion structure.
Why retained: spectacular visual feedback and deduction from subtractive illumination.
Failure hypothesis: inaccessible to color-vision-deficient players and expensive/ambiguous in 3D.
Attack: design redundant symbol/channel encoding and discrete renderer-independent logic; kill if color remains semantically mandatory.

### J. Misprint Press (#25)
Hook: printing plates only ink blank paper; infer a plate sequence from which colors managed to claim each region first.
Why retained: physical rule is simple, visible, and creates precedence constraints rather than arithmetic.
Failure hypothesis: puzzles reduce to topological sorting/order enumeration.
Attack: find at least four deduction families beyond pairwise precedence and test whether rotations/partial plates add real coupling.

### K. Stage Directions (#26)
Hook: arrange lights so actors are lit, silhouetted, or hidden exactly as seen from several named seats.
Why retained: combines readable stage language with multi-view constraints; strong presentation.
Failure hypothesis: too many predicates and geometric special cases make authoring/certification brittle.
Attack: freeze a tiny predicate vocabulary and compare depth against Casting Call; only one should survive if they converge.

### L. Edge of Evidence (#28)
Hook: reconstruct broken ceramics by reasoning about whether painted strokes should terminate at breaks or continue across joins—not by matching the picture.
Why retained: tactile restoration fantasy; edge semantics can create exact logical constraints; easy screenshot hook.
Failure hypothesis: either trivial edge matching or visual jigsaw perception overwhelms reasoning.
Attack: abstract fragments into edge-event signatures and prove puzzles with ambiguous local joins resolved by global line-continuity constraints.
