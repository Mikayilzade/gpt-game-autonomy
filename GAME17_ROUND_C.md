# FINALIST C11 — HOUSE RULES

## Exact store hook
**Play a tiny card game against friends who each remember one rule wrong — then make deliberately strange moves to figure out exactly what each person believes before you correct them.**

## 15-second trailer sequence
0–3s: cozy four-seat table, simple match-by-suit-or-number card game; one guest objects to a visibly legal move.
3–6s: player hovers three misconception chips under that guest; a second guest approves the same move.
6–10s: player deliberately draws despite having a playable card; three guests react differently and several hypothesis chips vanish.
10–13s: host gives one public ruling; two guests update, stubborn guest does not.
13–15s: final hand freezes with all four misconception portraits solved; copy: **“Don’t beat the game. Diagnose the table.”**

## Six-case vertical slice
### HR01 — One objection, one cause
One guest, two candidate misconceptions. Player makes one legal same-number/different-suit play. Proves reaction-as-evidence and keeps base-game teaching under two minutes.

### HR02 — A useful bad move
Two guests, three misconception candidates. The best diagnostic move is strategically worse in the base card game. Proves that the real agency is experimental play, not winning the fictional game.

### HR03 — Same reaction, different reason
Two rules apply to one move; two guests object for distinct misconceptions. A second isolating move separates them. Proves causal diagnosis rather than reaction counting.

### HR04 — Test before teaching
Correcting a guest changes later reactions. Player can accidentally destroy evidence. Proves information has temporal value and intervention changes the system.

### HR05 — Public ruling propagation
Three guests have flexible/stubborn update traits. One ruling corrects some but not all; later reaction from a corrected guest becomes evidence about another. Proves learning dynamics with one immutable base game.

### HR06 — Final-table synthesis
Four guests, four misconception families, one limited correction and a requirement to finish the hand without an unresolved rule dispute. Proves the full loop: diagnostic move -> reactions -> candidate update -> timed correction -> final verification.

## Tutorial-tax test
Before the first satisfying decision the player must understand:
1. base legality: match suit OR number;
2. draw ends turn;
3. guests may hold one wrong rule from a visible small set;
4. their objections/approvals follow their belief model, not canon;
5. the host may intentionally choose suboptimal legal plays to gather information.

This can fit in roughly 3–4 minutes using HR01 and one forced demonstration. However, the tax is irreducible: the player must first internalize an invented game's canon well enough to recognize another person's misconception. Icons help, but the concept necessarily teaches a game in order to teach the game-about-the-game.

**Tutorial verdict: PASS, with meaningful tax.** First satisfying decision can occur before five minutes, but this remains the finalist's largest structural risk.

## Content-exhaustion test
**Hour 1:** novelty comes from legality misconceptions and choosing tests that split candidate beliefs. Do not add new base games.

**Hour 3:** add interaction between two canon rules, evidence destruction by correction, and 2–3 bounded learning traits. Do not add dialogue deception, hidden personality stats or free-form NPC reasoning.

**Hour 5:** add cases where multiple misconceptions predict the same immediate reaction for different reasons, requiring isolation experiments; corrected guests can provide later control observations. Do not add second misconceptions per guest as the normal rule because the candidate space explodes.

**Hour 8:** mastery comes from minimizing diagnostic turns and preserving evidence while still completing table objectives. Optional challenge variants can alter test budget or guest update traits. Do not add deckbuilding, collectible cards, multiple rule sets or roguelite meta progression.

The system plausibly survives eight hours, but content authoring must continually discover fresh diagnostic structures inside one tiny rule engine. The risk is not lack of state space; it is that cases feel like permutations of the same four test moves.

## Full-product content target / demo subset
- 1 immutable 16-card fictional shedding game.
- 8 core misconception modules, each with explicit opposite/correction state.
- 4 learning traits: flexible, stubborn, requires demonstration, accepts only direct ruling.
- 30–36 authored cases in 6 chapters, target 6–8 hours first completion.
- 10 optional mastery cases emphasizing minimal-test solutions.
- Demo: HR01–HR06 equivalent, ~25–35 minutes, 4 misconception modules, 2 learning traits.

## Deterministic solvability / explanation / dead-state requirements
- Every NPC reaction is generated from the exact belief-model rules engine; no authored arbitrary objections.
- Every case ships with an exhaustive solver over legal host actions, rulings and belief-state transitions.
- Required diagnosis must be uniquely inferable within the declared action budget.
- If the player corrects away essential evidence and uniqueness becomes impossible, the game may certify `UNDERDETERMINED` only when the solver proves no continuation can separate remaining worlds.
- Explanation view must show `move feature -> guest believed rule -> reaction`, never merely “wrong answer.”
- A successful diagnosis is accepted only if all assigned belief models match the hidden case state.

## Implementation pre-mortem
1. **Base game is harder than expected.** Cut scoring entirely; keep only legality/turn/draw rules.
2. **Diagnostic actions converge to the same best move.** Remove weak misconception modules rather than adding new card mechanics; require each retained module to participate in at least two distinct optimal experiment structures.
3. **Character presentation grows into dialogue production.** Reactions remain icon + one short bark; no branching conversation.
4. **Belief-state UI becomes chip soup.** Cap normal play at four guests and four live candidate chips per guest; auto-hide impossible candidates.
5. **Case authoring becomes manual proof work.** Build an exhaustive case validator and candidate-distance report before content population; kill unsound cases rather than patch with exceptions.

## Player-comprehension pre-mortem
Strongest complaint: **“I have to learn a fake card game before I can solve the actual puzzle.”**

Mitigation must not disguise the fact. The base rules need to be tiny enough to learn from two visible legal examples. Canon rules stay pinned on the table throughout the entire game; no memorization test. Chapter progression cannot introduce another game. If external playtest still shows players spending more attention recalling canon than designing tests by case 3, this concept fails its empirical gate.

## Product comparison notes
- Screenshot/GIF: warm and human-readable, but still looks like a card game until reactions/hypothesis chips animate.
- Capsule/title: **House Rules** is weak as a final title in 2026 because the phrase is generic across many card/table products. A future dedicated naming pass would be mandatory.
- Controller/Deck: excellent; snapped card focus and portrait/chip navigation.
- Localization: medium; base rule wording and misconception wording must preserve exact logical distinctions.
- Likely value: $9.99–$12.99 if 6–8 polished hours and a strong demo; higher risks mismatch with small-table presentation.

## Round-C score
Hook 5 / tutorial 3 / depth 5 / hour-8 novelty 4 / causality 5 / production 5 / demo 5 / market distinctness 4 / controller+localization 4 / value legibility 4 = **44/50**.

**Round-C decision: RUNNER-UP.** Strong, compact, highly testable, but tutorial tax plus generic current card/“house rules” surface is a real product handicap.

# FINALIST C10 — THE QUEUE KNOWS

## Exact store hook
**Change signs and counter rules in a tiny service hall, watch customers choose their own queues, and use those choices to deduce what each person needs — even when the best experiment makes the line worse on purpose.**

## 15-second trailer sequence
0–3s: six anonymous customers enter; two counters, one `FREE` sign, three public possible customer heuristics.
3–6s: player flips the sign; three customers switch queues while two stay put.
6–9s: reason traces flash briefly — shortest wait, zero fee, familiar counter — and candidate-type chips collapse.
9–12s: player delays opening one counter; congestion unexpectedly sends one urgent customer away, exposing a confound.
12–15s: second configuration cleanly separates the crowd into three groups; copy: **“Make the queue reveal who is standing in it.”**

## Six-case vertical slice
### QK01 — Self-selection
Three customers, two known possible types: Routine chooses familiar A; Price-sensitive chooses the counter marked Free. Player chooses sign placement and watches a clean split. Proves visible intervention -> behavior -> inference.

### QK02 — Identical until tested
Urgent and Routine both currently choose A. Player changes one bounded feature so their predictions diverge. Proves experiment design rather than passive observation.

### QK03 — The experiment changes the measurement
Five customers. A strong Free sign attracts enough people to lengthen A, causing an Urgent customer to switch too. Proves endogenous congestion/confounding and establishes why naive maximum separation can fail.

### QK04 — Sequential cohort
Early arrivals commit before the player changes one sign. Their choices reveal which late-arrival intervention is safe. Proves alternating observation/control and time without real-time dexterity.

### QK05 — Pooling is enough
Player need not identify all six customers. Goal is to ensure only medical-priority type reaches Counter C while all other types may remain pooled. Proves operational separation rather than full classification every case.

### QK06 — Constrained diagnostic service
Eight customers, three counters, four candidate types, two interventions, service rule `no customer waits >4 ticks`. Player must create an informative split without violating service. Proves late-game trade between information value and operational consequence.

## Tutorial-tax test
Before the first satisfying decision the player needs only:
1. each candidate customer type follows one visible deterministic choice rule;
2. the player can alter one visible hall condition;
3. customers choose based on the resulting state;
4. their choice is evidence about which rule they follow.

QK01 can teach all four through direct motion and one-line reason traces. No invented board-game rules, symbolic notation, economy or story exposition is needed. First meaningful free decision can occur around minute 2–3.

The critical framing lesson arrives immediately: **your goal is not always shortest queues; sometimes you deliberately create a choice that tells you something.** QK02 should explicitly reward a temporarily worse-looking queue to lock this identity before management habits form.

**Tutorial verdict: STRONG PASS.** Lowest irreducible tutorial burden of the finalists.

## Content-exhaustion test
**Hour 1:** basic type separation using sign, fee and opening-time differences. The player learns to think in predicted choices, not throughput. Do not add upgrades, staff or money.

**Hour 3:** endogenous congestion creates confounding; early customers alter the conditions later customers see. Add sequential intervention timing and fixed service constraints. Do not add free-roaming pathfinding or real-time clicking.

**Hour 5:** pooling/separating goals vary: identify one special type, isolate two groups, prove a customer cannot be type X, or route a target type while preserving service. Same heuristics combine in different population compositions. Do not add dozens of customer types; normal campaign cap is 5 core heuristics plus bounded modifiers.

**Hour 8:** challenge comes from 3-counter interactions, limited intervention budgets, uncertain-but-public candidate sets per customer, and counterfactual planning across two cohorts. Do not add management meta, staffing trees, queue patience psychology, randomized AI or economy.

The same small rule vocabulary plausibly creates hour-8 novelty because the intervention changes the environment that determines later evidence. Long-run content must vary causal structure, not simply crowd size.

## Full-product content target / demo subset
- 5 core customer heuristics, all deterministic and short enough to state in one line:
  1. Price: choose lowest visible fee, tie -> shorter predicted completion.
  2. Urgent: choose shortest predicted completion, ignoring fee.
  3. Routine: choose familiar counter unless its predicted completion exceeds alternative by threshold T.
  4. Privacy: avoid counters/queues exposing a marked public category when a private counter is feasible.
  5. Convenience: minimize walking/transfer steps before completion, then shorter queue.
- 4 bounded hall levers: sign/fee flag, opening delay, service-category flag, capacity/service-time preset. Later cases may combine levers but do not introduce arbitrary one-off mechanics.
- 3 counters maximum in normal campaign; 10 customers maximum visible simultaneously.
- 36 authored campaign cases across 6 chapters; target 6–8 hours.
- 12 optional mastery cases with stricter intervention/service targets.
- Demo: QK01–QK06 equivalent, 25–35 minutes, 3 heuristics, 2 counters for first four cases, first 3-counter case as demo finale.

## Deterministic solvability / explanation / dead-state requirements
- Customer movement uses fixed queue slots and deterministic tick ordering; no continuous pathfinding determines puzzle outcomes.
- Every customer has a public candidate-type set. Hidden actual type is drawn only from that set and fixed at case start.
- Every choice produces an exact reason trace from the same evaluator used by the solver: observed hall state -> heuristic comparison -> tie-break -> chosen queue.
- Authored cases are validated across every hidden-world assignment allowed by their initial candidate sets.
- Required deduction must be uniquely supported by observations available before final commit; no statistical likelihood.
- If an intervention sequence makes the required goal impossible, a dead-state certificate requires exhaustive proof over remaining legal interventions, not a heuristic guess.
- A case may permit multiple successful intervention sequences. The validator proves goal satisfaction, not one intended route.
- Congestion prediction and service completion must use one canonical tick-order contract in later mechanical specification.

## Implementation pre-mortem
1. **Crowds become unreadable.** Cap visible population at 10 and use fixed lanes/queue slots; split large logical cohorts into grouped tokens only when they are behaviorally identical.
2. **It turns into throughput optimization.** Remove money, ratings, staff upgrades and generic efficiency scores. Every campaign case must contain an information/deduction goal; service constraints are secondary boundaries.
3. **Hidden types feel like guessing.** Candidate sets remain visible; post-choice reason trace is exact; case validator rejects worlds that cannot be distinguished within budget when identification is required.
4. **Simulation bugs/pathfinding dominate QA.** No free navigation. Customers teleport/animate between deterministic slot nodes after decisions; logical resolution is independent from animation.
5. **More content requires more customer archetypes.** Hard cap core heuristics at five; new cases must first use composition, timing, congestion and goal-form changes. A sixth heuristic requires explicit design review, not routine content authoring.

## Player-comprehension pre-mortem
Strongest complaint: **“Why am I making the queue worse on purpose? I thought I was supposed to serve people faster.”**

The fix is framing and scoring, not an extra tutorial paragraph. The first three cases never score raw speed. QK02 explicitly has a faster configuration that fails because it reveals nothing, and a slightly slower configuration that succeeds because it separates types. The objective banner should read an inference goal such as `Identify which two customers require Priority Service`, with wait limit shown only as a constraint. UI language avoids “efficiency,” “profit,” “rating,” and management-game stars.

## Product comparison notes
- Screenshot/GIF: strongest finalist. Sign flip -> visible queue reorganization communicates cause/effect even without reading the full rule set.
- Capsule/title: `The Queue Knows` is distinctive enough as a working title and directly implies the core mystery. No direct title collision surfaced in this search.
- Controller/Deck: strong. Focus between counters, levers and customers; fixed small room; no pointer precision.
- Localization: low-medium. Five heuristic rules and objective text require precision, but most state can be icon-backed and no dialogue is required.
- Likely value: **$11.99–$14.99 working range**, with $12.99 the Phase-3 planning MSRP if a 6–8 hour polished campaign plus demo is delivered. Exact commercial lock remains Phase 7.

## Round-C score
Hook 5 / tutorial 5 / depth 5 / hour-8 novelty 5 / causality 5 / production 4 / demo 5 / market distinctness 5 / controller+localization 5 / value legibility 4 = **48/50**.

**Round-C decision: WINNER.** It has the best combination of immediate legibility, systemic second-order depth, deterministic fairness, content economy and current product distinctness. The hidden-type risk is real but bounded by public candidate sets and exact reason traces.

# FINALIST C05 — ARCHIVE OF ALMOSTS

## Exact store hook
**Match anonymous failure reports to strange failed prototypes by designing the smallest possible experiments that make their hidden fault models behave differently.**

## 15-second trailer sequence
0–3s: four charming bench prototypes, three anonymous failure reports, two remaining tests.
3–6s: player toggles `lid open`, `preheated`, `short run`; four predicted mini-traces branch apart.
6–9s: test runs; actual trace follows one branch and candidate cards collapse from six to two.
9–12s: player disables overheating; a previously masked power-cycle fault suddenly appears.
12–15s: one report snaps onto the correct prototype; copy: **“Don’t guess the failure. Design the test.”**

## Six-case vertical slice
### AA01 — One discriminating control
Four one-fault prototypes, one test. Proves experiment selection over passive clue reading.
### AA02 — Redundant test trap
Two tests available; one tempting pair produces the same partition twice. Proves test budget and information value.
### AA03 — Temporal trace
Two faults share final output but differ mid-run. Proves observation timing.
### AA04 — Masking
Primary fault prevents secondary fault from manifesting; suppress one mechanism to reveal another. Proves intervention causal reasoning.
### AA05 — Negative control
The correct match depends on proving a condition does not matter under one model. Proves absence as evidence.
### AA06 — Shared rig
Two prototypes share bounded power/coolant line; testing one perturbs the other unless a control setting is used. Proves confounds/coupling without physics.

## Tutorial-tax test
The player must understand prototypes, public candidate fault models, bounded test conditions and that a good test separates hypotheses. AA01 can teach this in ~3 minutes. The conceptual tax is lower than House Rules but higher than Queue because the player must read predicted behavior models and understand a trace plot.

**Tutorial verdict: PASS.** The first satisfying decision is possible before five minutes, but physical staging must carry much of the explanation.

## Content-exhaustion test
**Hour 1:** simple partition tests across small finite fault sets.
**Hour 3:** temporal observation and redundant-test traps.
**Hour 5:** masking, negative controls and intervention order.
**Hour 8:** shared-rig confounds and multi-channel traces.

Do not add dozens of device families, realistic electronics, free-form lab equipment, chemistry recipes or narrative clue hunting. The strongest depth comes from causal experiment grammar.

The structural concern: optimal play can still become abstract “pick the condition that partitions candidate rows best.” Masking and confounds fight this, but the interface must make device causality primary rather than candidate-table optimization.

## Full-product content target / demo subset
- 3 modular prototype families sharing the same finite control vocabulary rather than 20 unrelated machines.
- 12–16 core fault modules plus bounded pairwise compounds.
- 30–36 authored cases, 6–8 hours target.
- Demo: AA01–AA06, 25–35 minutes, one device family plus one shared-rig finale.

## Deterministic solvability / explanation / dead-state requirements
- Same finite-state simulator powers visible test run, candidate predictions and validator.
- Exhaustive solver proves unique report/prototype mapping within test budget.
- Exact trace explains state transition, triggering condition and observed channel.
- Under-determined state can be certified only after every remaining legal test fails to separate required worlds.
- Multiple valid tests are allowed; no hidden intended solution.

## Implementation pre-mortem
1. Device art multiplies -> reuse modular shells and control widgets; cap families at three.
2. Candidate tables dominate -> default UI shows physical controls/traces; hypothesis matrix is optional detail.
3. Solver cost explodes with compounds -> cap simultaneous faults at two and bounded state depth.
4. Cases become obvious maximum-partition exercises -> reject cases whose optimal decision has no meaningful causal interpretation; require masking/control cases.
5. Trace visualization becomes engineering software -> maximum four channels, icon/state bands, no arbitrary analog plots.

## Player-comprehension pre-mortem
Strongest complaint: **“I understand the logic, but I feel like I am doing a lab worksheet.”**

Mitigation is tactile presentation and consequence, but presentation cannot become a crafting simulator. Prototypes should visibly click, vent, stall, blink and stop according to finite state; the player manipulates knobs/sockets directly. Candidate math stays secondary. If grey-box testing remains spreadsheet-like after physical controls are added, kill rather than compensate with story or art volume.

## Product comparison notes
- Screenshot/GIF: good when a test visibly causes a strange failure; weaker than Queue without motion context.
- Capsule/title: `Archive of Almosts` is evocative and distinctive but does not explain lab diagnostics by itself.
- Controller/Deck: good with snapped control sockets and timeline focus.
- Localization: low; concise fault descriptions and report text.
- Likely value: $9.99–$12.99 for a compact 6–8 hour puzzler; risk that players perceive it as a smaller logic workbook.

## Round-C score
Hook 4 / tutorial 4 / depth 5 / hour-8 novelty 5 / causality 5 / production 5 / demo 4 / market distinctness 5 / controller+localization 5 / value legibility 3 = **45/50**.

**Round-C decision: RUNNER-UP.** Best technical/validation ratio, but weaker emotional/product legibility than the winner and retains a nontrivial worksheet-collapse risk.
