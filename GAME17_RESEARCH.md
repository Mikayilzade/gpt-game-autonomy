### Market deductions used in this round
- **Demo-first legibility is mandatory.** A candidate should expose its core surprise inside 5–15 minutes and produce a GIF/trailer beat without lore explanation.
- **Do not compete on raw puzzle count.** Depth should come from interactions, inference, recombination, or player-authored plans.
- **Generic cozy is not differentiation.** Tactility/art can amplify a game but cannot be the only hook.
- **Avoid obvious 2026 surfaces:** generic roguelite deckbuilders, merge-score games, deterministic dungeon-resource optimization, Sokoban-with-one-twist, cozy assembly, co-op communication puzzles, dark-fantasy co-op/extraction.
- **Prefer single-player, low bespoke-content burden, deterministic or inspectable outcomes, and controller-feasible interaction.**
- **No precision/physics dependency.** Physical-looking systems may use discrete/stateful rules, but success cannot depend on dexterity, unstable rigid-body behavior, or pixel hunting.

## 3. Retained candidates — equal-format briefs

### C01 — NIGHT SHIFT SWITCHBOARD
**Hook:** Route a strange office's calls through the right sequence of operators, but every handled subject changes what that operator knows, believes, or is authorized to transfer next.
**Core interaction:** inspect caller need + operator cards; assign/transfer one call at a time; observe deterministic knowledge/permission updates.
**Depth source:** calls interact through operator state, order, shared facts, and transfer prerequisites; later cases require using an early “wrong-looking” conversation to prepare a later valid route.
**Demo moment:** a call rejected by every operator becomes routable after another caller causes two operators to exchange a fact.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** can become a spreadsheet of flags rather than a satisfying switchboard fantasy.

### C02 — SECOND-HAND MUSEUM
**Hook:** Build tiny exhibitions where visitors reinterpret each object through the labels and exhibits they already encountered.
**Core interaction:** choose a small set/order of exhibits and labels; simulate visitor interpretation states; inspect why each visitor formed a conclusion.
**Depth source:** semantic context, visitor priors, order effects, and intentionally ambiguous labels.
**Demo moment:** the same mundane object reads as “authentic,” “replica,” or “evidence” depending on the preceding room.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** semantic rules may feel arbitrary unless the interpretation grammar is exceptionally explicit.

### C03 — PLATFORM 4½
**Hook:** Design a tiny station timetable where passengers transfer according to visible human habits—coffee, stairs, crowd avoidance, familiarity—not an omniscient shortest path.
**Core interaction:** place/retime a bounded set of services and platform amenities; simulate passenger cohorts; inspect choice explanations.
**Depth source:** interacting cohorts, dwell times, platform attractiveness and missed-transfer cascades without real-time control.
**Demo moment:** moving a coffee kiosk, not a train, saves a connection because a cohort changes platform choice early.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** risks looking like transport management and requiring too much simulation/UI for puzzle-scale payoff.

### C04 — THE QUIET AUCTION
**Hook:** Win the objects you actually need by bidding against deterministic collectors whose hidden valuation rules you can infer and manipulate.
**Core interaction:** inspect lots/history, submit bounded sealed bids or bundles, resolve simultaneously, update public rival evidence.
**Depth source:** rival policy inference, complement/substitute lots, budget preservation, price-setting and deliberate losses.
**Demo moment:** bid on an unwanted decoy so a rival exhausts budget, then win the true target cheaply.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** hidden policies could feel like guesswork and auction arithmetic may intimidate players.

### C05 — ARCHIVE OF ALMOSTS
**Hook:** An R&D archive contains failed inventions and anonymous test reports; identify which prototype produced each failure by designing a few diagnostic re-tests.
**Core interaction:** compare candidate prototypes' public rule differences; choose a test condition; receive deterministic result; assign reports/prototypes.
**Depth source:** active information gathering, tests that partition candidate sets differently, compound faults, limited test budgets.
**Demo moment:** one cleverly chosen test eliminates five plausible prototypes at once.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** could become dry logic-grid deduction with little visual identity.

### C06 — AFTER THE APPLAUSE
**Hook:** Reset an entire theater between scenes by giving a tiny stage crew simultaneous orders whose role priorities cause useful chain reactions.
**Core interaction:** assign each worker a short ordered task card, press GO, watch a deterministic 20–40 second reset, then revise before committing the scene change.
**Depth source:** workers react to shared stage state, role priority, tool availability, safe handoffs and tasks that satisfy prerequisites for others.
**Demo moment:** one stagehand moves a bench, exposing a trapdoor exactly when another arrives with a prop, turning six explicit tasks into three instructions.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** resembles programming/automation and may require expensive animation/state readability.

### C07 — COMMON KNOWLEDGE
**Hook:** Solve negotiations by controlling not just who knows a fact, but who knows that everyone else knows it.
**Core interaction:** choose where/when to make bounded public/private statements; inspect layered knowledge ledger; trigger deterministic commitments when epistemic conditions become true.
**Depth source:** first/second-order knowledge, overhearing, acknowledgements, public announcements and deliberately withheld confirmation.
**Demo moment:** telling everyone separately fails; one public acknowledgement succeeds because it creates common knowledge.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** intellectually distinctive but extremely difficult to teach and visualize without feeling academic.

### C08 — TERMS AND CONDITIONS
**Hook:** Assemble a short contract where choosing one definition changes what several later clauses actually require.
**Core interaction:** choose/reorder clauses from small sets; inspect live plain-language consequences and counterpart acceptance conditions.
**Depth source:** scoped definitions, exceptions, precedence, cross-references and counterpart preferences; no free-form text.
**Demo moment:** a harmless definition of “delivery” silently makes a penalty clause apply to digital handoff too, flipping the negotiation.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** legal-text fantasy may be niche and reading burden may swamp play.

### C09 — LOST PROPERTY OFFICE
**Hook:** Return strange everyday objects to truthful claimants when every description is incomplete and category words mean different things to different people.
**Core interaction:** ask bounded clarifying questions, tag observable properties, assign objects to claims; receive contradiction explanations.
**Depth source:** category inference, relational descriptions, sets of similar objects and questions that simultaneously disambiguate several claims.
**Demo moment:** “the blue one” is not about color—the claimant's earlier language establishes “blue” as the transit line where it was lost.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** language ambiguity can cross the line from fair inference into author trickery/localization pain.

### C10 — THE QUEUE KNOWS
**Hook:** Configure a tiny service hall where customers choose lines by visible heuristics, then use the queues they form as information about who they are and what they need.
**Core interaction:** place a few public signs/service promises, open/close counters between rounds, simulate deterministic arrivals, infer types from resulting choices.
**Depth source:** mechanism design: signs both route customers and act as diagnostic tests; congestion changes later choices.
**Demo moment:** deliberately advertise a slower counter to separate urgency-sensitive customers from price-sensitive ones.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** optimization may dominate deduction, and crowd simulation may obscure causality.

### C11 — HOUSE RULES
**Hook:** Everyone at board-game night confidently remembers one rule differently; make diagnostic moves to discover each person's private ruleset before the final match.
**Core interaction:** choose legal-looking test moves in tiny fictional tabletop games; observe accept/object/pass reactions; infer each guest's misconception.
**Depth source:** experiment design, overlapping rule hypotheses, social observation, and tests whose outcome depends on multiple remembered rules.
**Demo moment:** a deliberately pointless move makes three guests react differently, revealing three rule models at once.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** requires several invented mini-rule systems and can balloon into content-authoring burden.

### C12 — THE SUBSTITUTE TEACHER
**Hook:** Get a class to the correct answer when students copy, challenge, or teach one another according to visible personalities—and some start with misconceptions.
**Core interaction:** choose who answers, pairs, or demonstrates; then deterministic student-to-student influence resolves.
**Depth source:** social propagation, confidence thresholds, source trust, misconception diagnosis and lesson order.
**Demo moment:** asking the wrong student first is optimal because their predictable mistake causes the skeptical student to challenge it publicly, correcting two others.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** could feel manipulative or thematically uncomfortable; needs abstraction and warmth.

### C13 — MINUTE HAND
**Hook:** A room of clocks disagrees; diagnose their deterministic faults by choosing when and how to observe them, then reconstruct the true time.
**Core interaction:** inspect clocks, advance controlled intervals, compare displayed changes, assign fault models from a bounded vocabulary.
**Depth source:** drift, skipped teeth, periodic stalls, swapped hands, reset offsets, coupled observations and limited measurement opportunities.
**Demo moment:** two clocks agree now but diverge after exactly one chosen interval, proving both faults and revealing true time.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** once fault vocabulary is learned, cases may reduce to repetitive algebra unless later interactions genuinely change inference structure.

### C14 — PUBLIC REHEARSAL
**Hook:** Fix a stage scene by editing only a few cue cards; actors know only their own cues and must self-coordinate without a director once rehearsal starts.
**Core interaction:** inspect local cue cards, replace/move a bounded number of cues, run deterministic rehearsal, inspect each actor's reason for acting/waiting.
**Depth source:** distributed local information, cue dependencies, simultaneous triggers, ambiguous cues, recovery cues and sparse edits.
**Demo moment:** changing one actor's line causes three others to enter, hand off a prop and trigger a lighting cue with no global script edit.
**Primary failure hypothesis:** risks converging on programming/sequence repair and could overlap Missing Step unless local-information behavior is truly central.

## 4. Round-A destructive protocol

All 14 candidates receive the same probes. No candidate may gain points from promised future mechanics not present in its brief.

### Scoring
Each dimension is **0–5**. Maximum = 50. Scores are comparative, not claims of market probability.

1. **10-second hook:** Can a store capsule/GIF communicate the surprising verb/result without lore?
2. **Minute-to-minute agency:** Does the player repeatedly make meaningful decisions rather than merely read, wait, or execute a solved answer?
3. **Second-order depth:** Do simple rules interact to create new reasoning, rather than only increasing list size?
4. **Fair causality:** Can failure/success be explained from visible rules with low trial-and-error dependence?
5. **Demo strength:** Can a 15–30 minute demo reach a memorable “I caused that” moment and stop at a natural appetite point?
6. **Hour-5/10 resistance:** Is there a credible route to sustained novelty without hundreds of bespoke rules/assets?
7. **Production proportionality:** Can a small team build the core with low/moderate art, animation, writing, localization and QA burden?
8. **Input/readability:** Mouse + controller/Deck can expose the full reasoning state without precision interaction or giant spreadsheets.
9. **Portfolio/market distinctness:** Avoids both #001–#016 identity collisions and obvious current crowded surfaces.
10. **Commercial legibility:** Screenshot/trailer/store text can sell the product as a game, not homework or a generic genre skin.

### Mandatory kill probes regardless of score
A candidate is killed in Round A if any one is true after concrete simulation:
- **Enumeration collapse:** optimal play is essentially checking every finite option and content depth scales mainly by adding options.
- **Opaque-rule dependence:** the designer must hide arbitrary behavior for the puzzle to work.
- **UI spreadsheet collapse:** a truthful interface needs tables/ledgers so dense that the fantasy disappears.
- **One-clever-trick collapse:** the demo reveal is strong but the fifth case is structurally the first case with more entities.
- **Production inversion:** making consequences understandable costs more than the core interaction is worth.
- **Portfolio collision:** once theme is removed, the central state transition is substantially a prior factory game's identity.
- **Market clone:** once theme is removed, the pitch is a familiar crowded loop plus a cosmetic modifier.

### Required Round-A artifact per candidate
For each C01–C14, next run must produce:
1. a **concrete 3–7 minute microcase** with initial state, player decisions and deterministic resolution;
2. the candidate's **case #5 escalation** showing what genuinely new reasoning appears;
3. a **50-point score** using the common rubric;
4. one **strongest kill argument** and one **strongest defense**;
5. explicit PASS / KILL.

Target after Round A: **6–8 survivors**, not a winner. If fewer than five honestly survive, do not rescue weak candidates; return to opportunity discovery for replacements.

## 5. Phase-1 conclusions

- 30 clean-slate seeds were generated before preference filtering.
- 16 were eliminated during broad-field and collision filtering; 14 enter equal Round A.
- No concept is selected and no Phase-3 canon exists.
- The most important unresolved risk across the field is **explainability versus abstraction**: several candidates gain depth from beliefs, knowledge, interpretation or local rules, but those same systems can become text-heavy. Round A must test concrete microcases rather than reward clever pitches.
- The second major risk is **automation/programming convergence** in C06/C14. They survive only because their proposed depth is role/local-information behavior, but Round A must kill them if the optimal mental model becomes ordinary scripting.
- Market evidence supports keeping a strong demo gate and rejecting generic cozy/Sokoban/roguelite/deterministic-dungeon surfaces; it does not justify choosing any current candidate yet.
