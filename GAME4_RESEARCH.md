# 1. Run objective

Build a fresh, portfolio-distinct candidate field using current 2026 discovery pressure, demo economics, direct-action readability, small-team production reality and long-session depth. Do not select a winner in this run.

The immediate target is:
- at least 30 genuinely different seeds;

- repeated verbs that can be understood in motion;
- cheap graybox falsification;
- no dependency on servers, multiplayer, photorealism, huge dialogue volume or procedural filler.

## 2.1 Demo competition is extreme
The June 2026 Next Fest dataset tracked 4,244 demos. Roughly the top 4% captured nearly 70% of modelled wishlist gains, while demo-player activity correlated with wishlist growth more strongly than Twitch exposure.

**Implication:** Game #004 must have a playable hook, not merely a clever pitch. A strong 10-second clip and a convincing 15–25 minute demo remain product requirements.

## 2.2 Generic simulation/job surfaces are crowded
Steam's current upcoming list visibly contains repair, farming, organizing, shop/job and management simulators alongside many action roguelites and horror titles.

**Implication:** do not select `ordinary occupation + tactile chores + upgrades` unless the actual repeated verb is exceptional. Familiar genre framing must not carry the concept.

## 2.3 Small indies can compete through specificity
Current GDC/editorial examples continue to reward highly specific physical verbs, absurd embodiment and instantly understandable transformations rather than broad feature lists.

**Implication:** a concept should ideally create its own trailer moment every few seconds through normal play.

## 2.4 Discovery pressure makes production efficiency more valuable, not less
Tools make it easier to ship games, which also raises supply. Cheap production is useful only when paired with an identity people can remember.

**Implication:** prefer one coherent mechanic with recombination depth over low-cost genre imitation.

# 3. Game #004 opportunity rules

Favor concepts that have:
1. one repeated 5–15 second action with immediate visible consequence;
2. a mute-video readable transformation;
3. depth from <=8 major rule families where plausible;
4. 5–10+ hour potential without huge bespoke content volume;
5. a one-week graybox that can genuinely kill the idea;
6. controller viability;
7. single-player/offline completeness;
8. a 15–25 minute demo with at least one independent player decision;
9. low dependence on realistic free physics;
10. clear distance from the previous three factory games.

Hard warnings:
- property-transfer clones;
- collision-consequence transfer;
- official-map/world rewriting;
- organism/cargo logistics as central fantasy;
- generic resize/gravity/magnet/echo/shadow puzzles without a new verb;
- conventional circuits interrupted by menus;
- slow plan/watch loops whose only depth is optimization;
- content whose novelty requires dozens of exceptions;
- freeform physics that cannot be predicted without trial spam.

# 5. Initial destructive triage

## Tier A — direct analogue/graybox research next

### A1 — G4C01 Seam Thief
Why alive: the actual verb is editing **adjacency**, not teleporting freely. Stitching two edges means every shortcut has a visible seam and potentially changes topology for multiple objects.

Must prove:
- seam endpoints can be selected with controller-friendly snaps;
- stitched adjacency creates more than portal routing;
- no universal `stitch start directly to goal` solution.

### A2 — G4C02 Missing Piece
Why alive: removing geometry and creating a same-shape absence elsewhere is visually unusual and naturally creates tradeoffs.

Must prove:
- shape matching remains readable rather than tedious;
- 5–6 shape families can create hour-3 depth;
- this is not merely block-placement after a fancy extraction animation.

### A3 — G4C03 Frame Job
Why alive: panel borders are physical world boundaries. Moving a border can change which panel's local rules/location owns an object.

Must prove:
- border movement has immediate consequences beyond rearranging a comic page;
- camera/UI remains readable on Deck;
- panels do not require bespoke narrative content.

### A4 — G4C04 One-Bullet Mason
Why alive: a construction anchor created through one action-shot has direct feel and clear clip language.

Must prove:
- one reusable shot does not become slow grapple placement;
- anchors support at least 3–4 distinct action/puzzle transformations;
- controller aim can be strongly snapped/assisted without deleting mastery.

### A5 — G4C05 Cutaway House
Why alive: one physical building piece can solve elsewhere only by making its source location vulnerable/incomplete.

Must prove:
- panel transport does not devolve into carrying a key-shaped block;
- structural consequences are readable and varied;
- traversal/backtracking does not dominate.

### A6 — G4C09 Negative Space Courier
Why alive: the playable space is what is **not** occupied by movable panels. One layout action changes route shape immediately and visually.

Must prove:
- works with controller and multiple aspect ratios;
- interface-native identity does not rely on desktop OS tricks;
- mature play is not generic sliding-block rearrangement.

### A7 — G4C11 Stagehand Zero
Why alive: scenery depth/layer changes can transform what is background decoration into collision geometry during live movement.

Must prove:
- depth ownership is instantly understandable;
- action remains deterministic and not perspective-trick trial-and-error;
- level authoring burden is bounded.

### A8 — G4C14 Threadbare Heist
Why alive: one finite thread can create topology, but each shortcut consumes visible path length and remains spatially embodied.

Must prove:
- input can use authored anchor snaps, not freehand drawing;
- one optimal straight-line shortcut does not dominate;
- action/stealth is not reduced to menu planning.

### A9 — G4C24 Debris Sculptor
Why alive: destruction is also the content source for construction. The player cannot spawn arbitrary blocks; every usable chunk must come from a real marked seam.

Must prove:
- deterministic chunk outcomes are satisfying enough without realistic chaos;
- debris inventory does not emerge;
- breaking more is not always better.

### A10 — G4C34 Single Cause
Why alive: extreme scope discipline. The player places one intervention, then watches a bounded deterministic machine chain. Strong clip/readability and easy sharing of alternate solutions.

Must prove:
- it is not boring plan/watch;
- the single intervention still contains a rich decision space;
- fast iteration/replay keeps agency high.

### A11 — G4C19 Soundproof Smuggler
Why alive: rather than using sound as detection meter, the player physically reshapes sound propagation with one movable absorber.

Must prove:
- no-audio accessibility retains full logic;
- stealth waiting is not dominant;
- sound geometry is predictable before movement.

### A12 — G4C28 Tension Window
Why alive: one bounded membrane can be the whole expressive tool—catch, redirect, launch, block, support.

Must prove:
- canonical outcomes can be simplified enough for reliable puzzles/action;
- feel remains satisfying with assist/snap rules;
- soft-body simulation does not become the project.

# 7. Tier C — reject as currently formulated

### G4C13 Borrowed Camera
Camera-framing interaction is established enough that `only framed objects are interactable` is not yet defensible.

### G4C16 False Floor
Directly transfers a binary property between locations and risks reproducing the property-transfer family already heavily explored/rejected in Game #003 discovery.

### G4C21 Shape Tax
Conserved resizing remains visually too close to established size-manipulation games, with extra geometry complexity rather than stronger identity.

### G4C27 Gravity Receipt
Too much history/echo bookkeeping for a movement verb that is not immediately better than established afterimage/recording mechanics.

### G4C29 Viewfinder Weight
Perspective/camera-based physical fixation has high comparison pressure and risks free-camera precision.

### G4C32 Suture Runner
Interesting action feel, but structurally too close to the previously explored in-motion system-editing family and needs a more distinct consequence.

### G4C33 Window Washer
The tactile action is clear, but the job-simulator cleaning surface is currently saturated and likely dominates perception.

### G4C35 Pocket Eclipse
Shadow manipulation remains too occupied to justify the current version without a more original second-order rule.

# 8. Run-1 lessons

1. **Topology manipulation** is the strongest fresh cluster in this batch, but must avoid becoming generic portal routing.
2. **Creation with a visible source cost** remains useful, but Game #004 should not repeat Game #003's portable-consequence identity or generic property conservation.
3. **Negative space and layer ownership** offer strong visual identities with lower simulation burden than free physics.
4. **Direct action helps discovery**, but aim/drawing precision must be controller-friendly and accessibility-safe.
5. **Deterministic destruction** is promising only if chunks have authored qualitative outcomes; realistic debris simulation is a production trap.
6. **Single-intervention games** can be extremely cheap and shareable, but plan/watch contamination must be attacked early.
7. **Audio-centered rules** require complete visual redundancy from the start; otherwise accessibility changes the game rather than presenting it.
8. The weakest concepts are those where the noun is unusual but the verb is still `toggle property`, `move camera`, `wait for timing`, `resize`, `clean`, or `use a portal`.

# 9. Phase-1 scoring model for Run 2

Score 1–5:
- Hook / mute-clip clarity — 15%
- Repeated core pleasure — 15%
- Systemic depth — 12.5%
- Hour-10 repetition resistance — 10%
- Scope fitness — 10%
- Graybox falsifiability — 10%
- Demo strength — 10%
- Tutorial burden — 5%
- Technical predictability — 7.5%
- Competitive whitespace — 5%

Hard-kill overrides:
1. a current game owns substantially the same noun + verb + fantasy;
2. fun needs unstable free physics;
3. one universal placement/action trivializes mature situations;
4. the muted clip looks like a familiar genre despite explanation;
5. depth requires >8–10 major mechanics or large exception sets;
6. accessibility adaptation removes the intended decision/motor skill;
7. first independent decision arrives too late for a 15–25 minute demo;
8. mature play becomes mostly waiting, carrying pieces back and forth, or opening configuration menus.
