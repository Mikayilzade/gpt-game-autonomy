## 2.2 Repetition-cluster audit
The 26 encounters can be grouped into roughly **18 materially distinct systemic signatures** before geometry/theme becomes the main novelty source.

Likely repetition clusters:
- T05/T11/T12 are all two-placement traversal relays, but differ in which archetype creates the commitment.
- T14/T15/T22/T24 all use mutation, but the player's location and direction of redistribution differ.
- T17/T18/T19 test repeated instances of one archetype and should not all appear consecutively.
- T25/T26 are synthesis encounters and should be spaced as late campaign/climax.

Campaign risk becomes noticeable above roughly **26–30 main encounters** under this vocabulary ceiling. The concept does not need 35–40 encounters to prove value.

## 2.3 Hour-5 defense
The strongest defense is that one anchor move causes **several simultaneous physical consequences**, and later play asks the player to value the same distribution differently because of traversal position and load mutation. The cognitive question is not simply “which socket is correct?” but “which temporary compromise do I want now, knowing what becomes unavailable next?”

The weak version still exists: if levels expose all sockets from one safe station, the game becomes a readable lookup table. That is a content-authoring failure and should later be validator-rejected rather than hidden by fuzzy feedback.

## 3.3 Hour-5 defense
The candidate's defense is embodied action: even when reasoning families repeat, launch/catch/transfer can remain satisfying. That is also its risk. If movement/catching feels only functional rather than pleasurable, repeated late-game encounters expose the narrower systemic signature set quickly.

The design cannot rescue that risk with a second tool or additional relationship type without abandoning the tournament thesis.

# 4. Hostile 20-minute experienced-player trace — Tension Budget

Assume all rules are known. Player deliberately tries to optimize away the signature verb and exploit restart/socket enumeration.

### Minutes 0–3 — enumerate every socket
The player approaches the first mature anchor and cycles 4 sockets rapidly. If all consequences are visible and the route can be completed from that same station, this defeats the puzzle.

**Result:** early/simple rooms remain vulnerable, but mature campaign layouts T05+ increasingly require physical traversal before the next useful anchor decision. The player can learn local consequences by enumeration, but cannot reveal post-traversal or post-mutation state from the initial safe station.

### Minutes 3–8 — choose extremes only
Player tests whether SLACK/HIGH always dominate TAUT.

**Result:** threshold-compromise states T03/T07/T09 require middle posture; extreme-only heuristic fails visibly.

### Minutes 8–13 — search for permanent best socket
Player attempts to leave anchor in one globally safe configuration.

**Result:** sequence and mutation states invalidate permanent parking. A content validator should later reject any mature encounter where one socket satisfies every completion-critical state.

### Minutes 13–17 — exploit restart
Player brute-forces a three-placement sequence using rapid restart.

**Result:** restart can shorten experimentation but does not collapse the state space to a single static choice because later states depend on player location and visible load mutation. Punishing restart is unnecessary.

### Minutes 17–20 — wait for machinery
Player tries to avoid anchor moves by waiting.

**Result:** authored loads do not cycle into alternate tension states by waiting. Passive wait yields almost nothing.

### Hostile verdict
The signature verb cannot be fully optimized away if mature content enforces traversal-separated decisions and visible mutations. The biggest exploit remains **safe socket enumeration**, which is authoring-detectable and can be rejected without hiding information.

# 5. Hostile 20-minute experienced-player trace — Zero-G Tool Orbit

### Minutes 0–4 — park tool indefinitely
Player chooses a track that repeatedly hits the progress target and waits.

**Result:** teaching states collapse, but mature states require player relocation and eventual recovery/transfer. Parking alone does not complete most late content.

### Minutes 4–8 — avoid catching until forced
Player leaves tool in orbit and tries to route around the room without recovery.

**Result:** several mature signatures still force catch because the same tool must serve another anchor/relationship. However, this confirms that many late puzzles are fundamentally “when/where must I catch the one tool?”

### Minutes 8–12 — exploit cycle waiting
Player waits for favorable periodic alignment rather than moving with the tool.

**Result:** some phase/order puzzles can be made easier through waiting. It does not usually invalidate the solution, but can reduce tension and make the action loop more passive than intended unless traversal windows are broad enough to encourage movement and short enough that repeated idle cycles are unattractive.

### Minutes 12–16 — brute-force track choice
With only Ring/Oval, player tries each track, observes which target/hazard it intersects, restarts if needed.

**Result:** track selection itself is highly brute-forceable. The candidate relies on **catch-position, hand-off and movement consequence** for mature depth, not on track-selection complexity.

### Minutes 16–20 — exploit generous magnetic catch
Accessibility-safe capture zones are intentionally forgiving. Experienced player tries to spam catch near an anchor/transfer zone rather than plan precise arrival.

**Result:** too-generous automatic capture risks turning hand-off into a trivial proximity trigger; too-tight capture makes solved puzzles fail on dexterity. Unlike Tension's main exploit, this tradeoff is not purely authoring-level — it is a core feel parameter.

### Hostile verdict
Parking does not dominate mature paper content, but the experienced-player attack reveals a deeper tension: the same generous capture that protects puzzle clarity can weaken catch-position planning, while tighter capture raises execution failures. This is a genuine product-level tuning risk.

# 6. Controller / accessibility / implementation-risk audit

## 6.1 Tension Budget existential gate — nonnumeric readability
Required controller path:
- approach anchor;
- grab;
- move along a snapped rail/socket axis;
- preview/see all connected loads respond continuously or at clear state boundaries;
- release.

Accessibility strengths:
- low precision requirement;
- no reaction-time dependency;
- can use shape/posture + line thickness + motion direction rather than color;
- reduced-motion mode can shorten transitions while preserving simultaneous before/after highlighting;
- controller remap is trivial.

Main risk:
- players may perceive SLACK/TAUT/HIGH as arbitrary hidden values rather than physical redistribution.

Implementation containment:
- all cable/load states are discrete;
- every relevant consequence can be shown directly in world geometry;
- no realistic rope simulation is required;
- if a cable state cannot be distinguished at normal camera scale, that authored state is invalid.

Risk character: **readability/design presentation risk, low execution risk**.

## 6.2 Zero-G Tool Orbit existential gate — execution reliability
Required controller path:
- move with thrust/damped locomotion;
- enter anchor capture zone;
- select Ring/Oval;
- release;
- traverse independently;
- arrive in a recovery zone;
- catch/transfer.

Accessibility strengths:
- authored tracks make trajectory preview deterministic;
- no ballistic aiming is required;
- target/catch regions can be enlarged;
- whole-simulation-speed assist is possible later if ordering is preserved.

Main risks:
- zero-G locomotion itself may be less immediately comfortable than ordinary top-down movement;
- capture generosity trades against the cognitive value of catch planning;
- missed catches are disproportionately frustrating because they occur after the player already understood the solution;
- reduced-motion support cannot remove the actual moving second actor without changing gameplay.

Implementation containment helps but cannot eliminate the central feel tradeoff.

Risk character: **core control/feel risk with medium execution burden**.

# 7. One-week empirical kill prototype — implementation-ready definition

No production code is created in the factory. These are later empirical instructions only.

## 7.1 Tension Budget prototype
### Build scope
One graybox facility containing:
- one avatar with ordinary top-down movement;
- one anchor on four snapped sockets;
- three cables;
- one Lift, one Counterweighted Gate, one Flexible Span;
- discrete SLACK / TAUT / HIGH postures;
- one objective that visibly removes the Gate counterweight from the shared budget;
- three test encounters: teaching, traversal sequence, mutation/return inversion.

### Instrument/observe
For 8–12 unfamiliar players:
- consequence-prediction accuracy after first teaching encounter;
- number of blind socket cycles before committing;
- whether players request numbers/gauges/graph view;
- time from grab to intentional release;
- ability to explain collateral tradeoff in plain language;
- whether mature solution requires at least two separated anchor decisions.

### PASS
- median prediction accuracy after teaching >= ~75%;
- most players can say a version of “pulling tension here takes/changes it elsewhere” without a numeric explanation;
- blind full enumeration is not the dominant solving method in mature tests;
- anchor action is completed confidently in <= ~2–3 seconds once reached;
- no rope simulation is needed to make cause/effect believable.

### FAIL
- players repeatedly ask for tension numbers or a detached diagram;
- cable posture is not readable at gameplay zoom;
- mature puzzles are routinely solved faster by cycling every socket than by reading the world;
- anchor feels like a slow menu selector rather than a physical mechanism.

## 7.2 Zero-G Tool Orbit prototype
### Build scope
One compact graybox set containing:
- one avatar with damped/capped zero-G movement;
- one tool;
- two anchors;
- Ring + Oval tracks;
- one Strike Target;
- one periodic Intercept Hazard;
- generous deterministic Catch/Transfer zones;
- three test encounters: track teaching, launch/traverse/catch, transfer + mutation/return.

### Instrument/observe
For 8–12 unfamiliar players using controller:
- intentional track-selection success;
- catch success when player reaches the correct planned region;
- solved-solution failures caused by movement/catch execution;
- idle orbit cycles while player waits;
- whether players spam capture rather than plan arrival;
- ability to explain tool as persistent second actor rather than projectile.

### PASS
- >=90% catch success once player is in the correct planned recovery region after short onboarding;
- understood mature solutions rarely fail from controller precision;
- players move during orbit rather than wait through repeated cycles;
- catch/transfer still requires meaningful spatial planning despite generous capture;
- no realistic orbital physics or free aim is needed.

### FAIL
- solved puzzles fail frequently because of missed catch/zero-G drift;
- enlarging capture enough to fix failures makes catch planning trivial;
- players commonly stand and wait multiple cycles;
- product reads as “choose which circle hits a button.”

# 9. Final equal-destruction decision

## Why Tension Budget wins
The difference is now materially larger than the one-point Run-2 spread.

1. **Campaign-scale depth survives more comfortably.** Under equally frozen vocabularies, Tension produces about 18 materially distinct systemic signatures before cosmetic geometry becomes the main novelty source; Orbit produces about 15 and shows stronger catch/transfer clustering.
2. **Its dominant-strategy problem is more containable.** Safe socket enumeration can be rejected through encounter construction/validation while keeping all consequences honest. Orbit's hardest tradeoff — forgiving catch versus meaningful catch planning — is intrinsic to the core control loop.
3. **Lower execution burden protects the puzzle identity.** A player who understands Tension is unlikely to fail because of dexterity. Orbit can still become excellent, but its action feel must clear a narrower tuning corridor.
4. **Current market position is cleaner.** Broad orbit/gravity play is active and familiar. The narrow conserved-tension redistribution thesis remains less occupied in current evidence.
5. **Implementation and accessibility are safer without making the game less itself.** Discrete cables/loads remain deterministic and world-readable in principle; accessibility can strengthen motifs without changing rules.
6. **The losing candidate remains genuinely strong.** Zero-G Tool Orbit is not killed as a bad idea. It loses this tournament on risk-adjusted product confidence, not because the hook or prototype potential is weak.

## Tournament winner
**G5C02 — TENSION BUDGET**

This is the selected **Game #005 concept** for Phase 3. `Tension Budget` remains a working concept label, not a frozen commercial title.

### Frozen selection core carried to Phase 3
- physical top-down/systemic puzzle baseline;
- player directly moves one anchor;
- a fixed discrete tension budget redistributes among visible cable-connected loads;
- consequence is collateral: helping one mechanism can weaken another;
- cable state language remains SLACK / TAUT / HIGH;
- baseline reusable load vocabulary remains Lift / Counterweighted Gate / Flexible Span;
- no numeric tension UI, graph editor, rope construction or realistic rope simulation;
- mature play must separate anchor decisions through traversal and/or visible load mutation so static safe socket enumeration does not become the game;
- low execution burden and controller viability are part of the product advantage;
- one-week empirical readability/tactility gate remains mandatory later.

### Reserve
**G5C37 Zero-G Tool Orbit** becomes the strongest Game #005 tournament reserve. Do not merge its mechanics into Tension Budget.
